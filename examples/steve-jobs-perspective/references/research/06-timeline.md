# Steve Jobs: A Complete Life Timeline (1955–2011)

> Research date: 2026-04-05
> Sources: English Wikipedia, Britannica, CNN, NPR, ABC News, Biography.com, Macworld, FastCompany, the Library of Congress, and others

---

## Chapter 1: Early Years (1955–1976)

### Birth and adoption

- **February 24, 1955**: Steven Paul Jobs was born in San Francisco, California. His biological father was Abdulfattah “John” Jandali, a Syrian graduate student in political science. His biological mother was Joanne Schieble, a graduate student at the University of Wisconsin. They were unmarried. Schieble's father opposed her marrying an Arab man, so they placed the child for adoption.
- **1955**: Paul Jobs, a machinist and used-car dealer, and Clara Jobs, a bookkeeper, adopted him. The family settled in Mountain View, California.
- Jobs later learned he was adopted, which became an important part of his psychological background. Paul Jobs assured him, “You were specially chosen.”

### Upbringing

- He grew up in the heart of Silicon Valley, in Mountain View and Los Altos, where many neighbors were Hewlett-Packard engineers.
- Paul Jobs's garage became Steve's first “laboratory.” His father taught him to take apart and reassemble electronics and car parts.
- His **obsession with craftsmanship** came from his adoptive father. When Paul Jobs refinished furniture, he insisted that the back be perfect too, even where nobody could see it.

### School and early interests

- **1968 (age 13)**: He called HP CEO Bill Hewlett to ask for parts. He got both the parts and a summer internship.
- **About 1969–1970**: At Homestead High School, he met Steve Wozniak, who was 18 when Jobs was 13. They became friends over their shared enthusiasm for electronics.
- **1971**: He and Wozniak built the Blue Box, an illegal device that enabled free long-distance calls. Jobs handled sales and Wozniak handled the technology. It was their first business venture together.
- **1972**: He graduated from Homestead High School.

### Reed College and spiritual exploration

- **Fall 1972**: He entered Reed College in Portland, Oregon.
- **Late 1972/early 1973**: He dropped out after one semester, feeling it was a waste for his parents to spend their savings on his education while he didn't know what he wanted to do. He continued auditing classes on campus for 18 months.
- **Auditing calligraphy**: Robert Palladino's class sparked a lasting interest in type and layout that directly influenced Macintosh typography a decade later. This became a central example of Jobs's philosophy of “connecting the dots.”
- **1974**: He returned to California and worked as a technician at Atari. That year he and his friend Daniel Kottke spent seven months on a spiritual journey in India, where he encountered Zen Buddhism.
- **1974–1975**: He began practicing at the Los Altos Zen Center under Kobun Chino Otogawa. Zen influenced his product philosophy throughout his life: simplicity, focus, and intuition.

### Working with Wozniak again

- **1975**: Wozniak began attending the Homebrew Computer Club and designing a personal computer.
- **Late 1975–early 1976**: Jobs saw the commercial potential of Wozniak's hand-built circuit board. Wozniak wanted to share the design for free, but Jobs persuaded him to start a company together.

## Chapter 2: Founding Apple (1976–1985)

### Starting in the garage

- **April 1, 1976**: Steve Jobs, 21, Steve Wozniak, 25, and Ronald Wayne, 41, founded Apple Computer, Inc. Wayne held a 10% stake but sold it back 12 days later for $800.
- Initial capital came from Jobs selling his Volkswagen van for $1,500 and Wozniak selling his HP-65 calculator for $500.
- **Apple I**: A hand-assembled bare circuit board priced at $666.66. It was sold mainly through the local computer store The Byte Shop. About 200 units were produced.

### Apple II and commercial takeoff

- **January 1977**: Apple Computer was formally incorporated.
- **April 1977**: Apple II debuted at the West Coast Computer Faire. It was the first personal computer aimed at mainstream consumers and included color display capability, a keyboard, and a plastic case.
- **1977**: Mike Markkula joined, invested $250,000, and brought business experience, becoming Apple's third key figure.
- **May 17, 1978**: Lisa Brennan-Jobs was born to Chrisann Brennan. Jobs denied paternity and even claimed in court papers that he was sterile.
- **1979**: At Xerox PARC (Palo Alto Research Center), Jobs saw a demonstration of a graphical user interface (GUI) and mouse and was deeply impressed. The visit directly led to the Lisa and Macintosh projects.
- **December 12, 1980**: Apple went public at $22 per share, closed its first day at $29, and reached a market value of $1.78 billion. Jobs, just 25, was worth more than $250 million.
- **1980**: A DNA test established that Lisa was Jobs's daughter. He began, reluctantly, to pay child support.

### The Macintosh project

- **1979**: Jef Raskin started the Macintosh project, envisioning a low-cost, easy-to-use computer.
- **1981**: After being sidelined in the Lisa project, Jobs took over Macintosh and redefined it as a product that would change the world.
- **1983**: Jobs convinced Pepsi president John Sculley to become Apple's CEO with his famous question: “Do you want to sell sugar water for the rest of your life, or do you want to change the world?”
- **January 22, 1984**: The Ridley Scott-directed “1984” ad aired during the third-quarter break of Super Bowl XVIII, becoming one of advertising's most iconic works.
- **January 24, 1984**: Macintosh launched at Apple's annual shareholder meeting at the Flint Center at De Anza College. Jobs pulled the Mac from a cloth bag, and the screen displayed “insanely great.” Its starting price was $2,495.

### Power struggle and ouster

- **1984–1985**: Macintosh sales fell short of expectations, cooling quickly after the initial enthusiasm. The conflict between Jobs and Sculley intensified.
- **May 1985**: Backed by Arthur Rock, Sculley proposed a reorganization that would move Jobs out of the Macintosh division.
- **May 24, 1985**: Apple's board voted in favor of Sculley's plan, and Jobs lost all operational responsibilities.
- **September 17, 1985**: Jobs submitted his resignation to Apple's board, took five senior Apple employees with him, and announced plans to start a new company.

**Turning point in his thinking**: Being pushed out of the company he founded was the biggest blow of Jobs's life. He later called it the best thing that ever happened to him. It gave him back the freedom of a founder and a beginner's mindset.

## Chapter 3: The “Exile” Years (1985–1997)

### NeXT Computer

- **September 1985**: Jobs founded NeXT, Inc. to build high-end workstations for higher education and business.
- **October 12, 1988**: The NeXT Computer, later called the NeXT Cube, debuted at San Francisco's symphony hall. It cost $6,500, with the total higher if the monitor was purchased separately, and featured the most advanced technology of the time.
- The operating system, NeXTSTEP, became NeXT's real legacy: object-oriented programming, Objective-C, and development tools. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web on a NeXT computer.
- **1990**: The lower-priced NeXTstation launched but still failed to reach the mass market.
- **1993**: NeXT stopped making hardware after selling only about 50,000 units in total. It became a software-only company focused on NeXTSTEP/Intel.
- **1994**: NeXT posted its first annual profit, $1.03 million.

### Pixar and the animation revolution

- **February 3, 1986**: Jobs bought Lucasfilm's Computer Graphics Division from George Lucas for $5 million, invested another $5 million, and became Pixar's chairman and major shareholder.
- **1986**: Pixar's short *Luxo Jr.* was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film, showing the potential of computer animation.
- **1988**: *Tin Toy* became the first computer-animated film to win the Academy Award for Best Animated Short Film.
- **May 1991**: Pixar signed a deal with Disney to make three computer-animated feature films together.
- **March 18, 1991**: Jobs married Laurene Powell at the Ahwahnee Hotel in Yosemite National Park. His Zen teacher Kobun Chino Otogawa officiated. The couple had met at a talk at Stanford's business school.
- **September 1991**: Their eldest son, Reed Paul Jobs, was born.
- **1995**: Their daughter Erin Siena Jobs was born.
- **November 22, 1995**: *Toy Story* was released, becoming the first feature film made entirely with computer animation. Its worldwide box office was $373 million.
- **November 29, 1995**: Pixar went public on NASDAQ under “PIXR” at $22 per share. In the first half hour, it climbed from $28 to $45, surpassing Netscape as the year's biggest IPO. Jobs's stake was worth around $1.2 billion.
- **February 25, 1997**: Pixar and Disney signed a new 10-year, five-film deal with equal profit sharing and equal billing for their brands.

**Turning point in his thinking**: The exile years were central to Jobs's maturation as a leader. At NeXT he learned not to pursue “insanely great” at the expense of commercial reality. At Pixar he learned to let specialists such as John Lasseter and Ed Catmull do what they did best. Laurene and the children gave his life an anchor.

### Changes in family relationships

- **Late 1980s–1990s**: Jobs gradually built a relationship with his daughter Lisa, who for a time moved in with Jobs and Laurene.
- **1998**: His youngest daughter, Eve Jobs, was born.
- The relationship remained complicated. Lisa Brennan-Jobs's 2018 memoir *Small Fry* revealed both tensions and reconciliations between father and daughter.

## Chapter 4: Return to Apple (1997–2007)

### Saving Apple from near-bankruptcy

- **December 20, 1996**: Apple announced it would buy NeXT for $429 million. Jobs returned as an informal adviser while Gil Amelio was CEO.
- **July 9, 1997**: Apple's board removed Amelio as CEO.
- **September 16, 1997**: Jobs was named interim CEO, jokingly called iCEO, with a symbolic annual salary of $1.
- **1997**: Apple launched “Think Different,” an advertising campaign saluting “the crazy ones,” including Einstein, Gandhi, and Picasso. It was more than advertising: it proclaimed the return of Apple's spirit.
- **August 6, 1997**: At Macworld Boston, Jobs announced that Microsoft would invest $150 million in Apple. The audience booed, but the deal helped Apple through its financial crisis.
- **1997–1998**: He drastically cut the product lineup, ending Newton, Cyberdog, OpenDoc, and other projects. Dozens of product lines were reduced to a four-quadrant grid: consumer/professional × desktop/notebook.
- **November 10, 1997**: Apple launched its Online Store, making it possible for customers to customize and buy Apple products directly.

### iMac and a design revival

- **January 8, 1998**: Apple announced it had returned to profitability.
- **May 6, 1998**: Apple unveiled the iMac G3, Jony Ive's all-in-one in translucent candy colors. It was the first signature product after Jobs's return.
- **August 15, 1998**: iMac went on sale and became Apple's fastest-selling product to date. It redefined the computer as something that could be beautiful and fun.
- **1999**: Apple released iMacs in five colors and a Power Mac G3 for professional users.
- **June 1999**: It introduced iBook, a consumer laptop.

### The digital-hub strategy

- **January 5, 2000**: At Macworld Apple revealed its strategy for Mac OS X, an entirely new operating system based on NeXTSTEP technology. Jobs formally dropped “interim” from his CEO title.
- **March 24, 2001**: Mac OS X shipped.
- **May 19, 2001**: The first Apple retail stores opened simultaneously in Tysons Corner, Virginia, and Glendale, California. Analysts generally predicted failure, and were later proved badly wrong.
- **October 23, 2001**: Apple introduced the original iPod, “1,000 songs in your pocket,” for $399. It initially supported only the Mac.
- **January 2001**: Apple released iTunes for Mac.
- **April 28, 2003**: The iTunes Music Store launched. It sold one million songs in its first week, proving that people were willing to pay for music. The 99-cent-per-song model overturned the music industry.
- **October 2003**: iTunes for Windows launched, opening the iPod ecosystem to PC users.

### iPod's dominance

- **January 2004**: The iPod mini debuted, smaller, more stylish, and cheaper at $249.
- **January 2005**: The screenless iPod shuffle debuted at $99.
- **September 2005**: The exceptionally thin iPod nano replaced the iPod mini.
- **October 2005**: Apple introduced the video-capable iPod and launched video on the iTunes Store.
- **2006**: iPod accounted for roughly 75% of the US digital music player market.

### The merger with Disney

- **January 24, 2006**: Disney announced it would acquire Pixar in an all-stock deal valued at $7.4 billion. Jobs became Disney's biggest individual shareholder, with around 7%, and joined its board.

**Turning point in his thinking**: The Jobs who returned was not the man who left in 1985. He had learned (1) to focus by cutting 90% of the product lineup, (2) to work with top talent rather than fight it, (3) that design was the soul of a product, not decoration, and (4) that the retail experience mattered. The run of successes from the iMac to the iPod proved how much he had changed.

## Chapter 5: The Peak Years (2007–2011)

### iPhone: Reinventing the phone

- **January 9, 2007**: Jobs introduced the iPhone at Macworld with the famous opening, “Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone.” He described it as three devices in one: a widescreen iPod, a revolutionary phone, and a breakthrough internet device.
- **June 29, 2007**: The original iPhone went on sale in the US at $499 for 4 GB and $599 for 8 GB. Customers lined up outside Apple Stores.
- **July 11, 2008**: The iPhone 3G launched with 3G connectivity and a lower price of $199.

### App Store: Creating a new economy

- **July 10, 2008**: The App Store went live with 500 apps, creating a new ecosystem for software distribution and the developer economy.
- The App Store logged over 10 million downloads in its first weekend.
- By 2011 it had more than 500,000 apps and over 18 billion total downloads.

### MacBook Air and the evolution of laptops

- **January 15, 2008**: Jobs pulled a MacBook Air out of a standard document envelope and declared it the world's thinnest notebook.

### iPad: Defining a new category

- **January 27, 2010**: Jobs introduced the iPad at San Francisco's Yerba Buena Center.
- **April 3, 2010**: The iPad went on sale, selling more than 300,000 units on its first day. Before then, the tablet category had widely been considered doomed.
- **June 7, 2010**: The iPhone 4 launched with the Retina display.
- **March 2, 2011**: Already on medical leave, Jobs appeared in person to unveil the iPad 2. It was his final major product launch.

### iCloud and the ecosystem

- **June 6, 2011**: Jobs introduced iCloud at WWDC in his final public keynote. He made a special return from medical leave to announce it, showing his foresight about cloud computing.

### Apple headquarters

- **June 7, 2011**: Jobs made his last public appearance before the Cupertino City Council. He presented the designs for Apple Park, or Apple Campus 2, and compared it to a spaceship that had landed. He did not live to see it completed.

## Chapter 6: Health and Legacy (2003–2011 and After)

### Cancer diagnosis and treatment

- **October 2003**: A kidney CT scan unexpectedly found a pancreatic tumor. Doctors first thought it was conventional pancreatic cancer, which is usually fatal. They later identified a rare pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (pNET), treatable with surgery.
- **October 2003–July 2004 (about nine months)**: Jobs refused surgery and tried alternative approaches: a vegetarian diet, acupuncture, herbs, and spiritual healing. The decision was later widely thought to have possibly delayed treatment beyond its best window.
- **July 31, 2004**: At Stanford University Medical Center he underwent a pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure), removing portions of the pancreas, stomach, and small intestine as well as the gallbladder.
- **August 1, 2004**: Jobs told Apple employees by email that he had a very rare form of pancreatic cancer and that surgery had succeeded without a need for chemotherapy or radiation.
- **June 12, 2005**: At Stanford's commencement he gave his famous “Stay Hungry, Stay Foolish” address. He told three stories about connecting the dots, love and loss, and death. It became one of the most quoted speeches of the internet age.

### Declining health

- **Mid-2008**: Jobs had visibly lost weight, prompting widespread speculation. Apple described it as a common minor ailment.
- **January 5, 2009**: Jobs announced that his “hormonal imbalance” was more complicated than expected.
- **January 14, 2009**: Jobs announced medical leave. Tim Cook took over day-to-day operations.
- **April 2009**: Jobs received a liver transplant at Methodist University Hospital in Memphis.
- **Late June 2009**: Jobs returned to Apple's offices.

### Final years

- **January 17, 2011**: Jobs announced a second medical leave without a return date. Tim Cook again handled operations.
- **March 2, 2011**: Jobs returned from leave to present the iPad 2, his final product launch.
- **June 6, 2011**: Jobs gave his final WWDC keynote and introduced iCloud.
- **June 7, 2011**: In his last public appearance he presented the Apple Park plan to the Cupertino City Council.
- **August 24, 2011**: Jobs resigned as Apple CEO, writing to its board, “I have always said if there ever came a day when I could no longer meet my duties and expectations as Apple's CEO, I would be the first to let you know. Unfortunately, that day has come.” Tim Cook succeeded him as CEO, and Jobs became chairman.

### Death

- **October 5, 2011**: Steve Jobs died at home in Palo Alto, California, at the age of 56, from respiratory failure caused by metastatic pancreatic cancer. His wife Laurene, children, and sisters were with him.
- His last words, according to his sister Mona Simpson's memorial account, were “Oh wow. Oh wow. Oh wow.”
- **October 16, 2011**: Apple held an employee memorial at its Cupertino headquarters.
- **October 19, 2011**: A private funeral was held at Stanford's Memorial Church.

### Posthumous impact and legacy

- **2012**: Walter Isaacson's authorized biography *Steve Jobs* was published. Jobs had participated in interviews during his lifetime but asked for publication after his death.
- **2013**: Construction of Apple Park began. It opened in 2017.
- **2015**: The movie *Steve Jobs* was released, written by Aaron Sorkin, directed by Danny Boyle, and starring Michael Fassbender.
- **2018**: His daughter Lisa Brennan-Jobs published *Small Fry*, a memoir about her complicated relationship with her father.
- **2022**: Laurene Powell Jobs's Emerson Collective became one of the world's most influential philanthropic and investment organizations.
- In the decade after Jobs's death, Apple's market value grew from about $350 billion to over $3 trillion, making it the world's most valuable company.

## Appendix: Turning Points in Jobs's Thinking

1. **Adoption** (1955) → The belief that he was chosen became a source of drive.
2. **Dropping out of Reed College** (1973) → Follow curiosity instead of a prescribed path.
3. **India and Zen Buddhism** (1974) → The philosophical roots of simplicity, intuition, and focus.
4. **Xerox PARC visit** (1979) → The GUI was the future, and user experience mattered more than technical specifications.
5. **Being pushed out of Apple** (1985) → “The best thing that ever happened to me” gave him a chance to begin again.
6. **Pixar and family** (1986–1997) → He learned to let go, trust the team, and balance life.
7. **Returning to Apple** (1997) → The power of focus: saying no matters more than saying yes.
8. **Cancer diagnosis** (2003) → “Remembering that you are going to die is the best way I know to avoid the trap of thinking you have something to lose.”
9. **Stanford commencement address** (2005) → “Stay Hungry, Stay Foolish,” a public statement of his philosophy of life.
10. **iPhone launch** (2007) → Proved that one person could redefine an industry more than once.
