This file was added for the v1→v2 upgrade. Earlier notes 01–06 (2026-04-05) were left unchanged. This file records three things: new material found in the follow-up, errors in the earlier notes and the previous SKILL.md, and a summary of findings that differ from the prior assessment.

## I. Primary material found in the follow-up

### 1. Fortune interview, March 2008 (Betsy Morris, Kona, Hawaii)

Transcript: https://allaboutstevejobs.com/verbatim/interviews/fortune_2008 · Originally published by Fortune on 2008-03-17 as “What makes Apple golden.” Primary.
This is the most detailed account he gave of his own working methods. The earlier notes quoted only the line on “focus”.

- [He said] Monday reviews: “So what we do every Monday is we review the whole business. We look at what we sold the week before. We look at every single product under development, products we're having trouble with, products where the demand is larger than we can make... I put out an agenda -- 80% is the same as it was the last week, and we just walk down it every single week. We don't have a lot of process at Apple, but that's one of the few things we do just to all stay on the same page.”
- [He said] Delegation: “When you hire really good people you have to give them a piece of the business and let them run with it. That doesn't mean I don't get to kibitz a lot... I want [them] making as good or better decisions than I would. So the way to do that is to have them know everything, not just in their part of the business, but in every part of the business.”
- [He said] His day-to-day work: “my job is to work with sort of the top 100 people... when a good idea comes, you know, part of my job is to move it around, just see what different people think, get people talking about it, argue with people about it, get ideas moving among that group of 100 people”
- [He said] Hiring: “competence is the ante... the real issue for me is, Are they going to fall in love with Apple?” “I've participated in the hiring of maybe 5,000-plus people in my life.” “I ask everybody that: 'Why are you here?' The answers themselves are not what you're looking for. It's the meta-data.”
- [He said] Responding to his reputation for being demanding: “My job is to not be easy on people. My job is to make them better.”
- [He said] The full context of “no market research”: “We do no market research. We don't hire consultants. The only consultants I've ever hired in my 10 years is one firm to analyze Gateway's retail strategy so I would not make some of the same mistakes they made.” In the adjacent passage: “I mean, the first few hundred customers were us... We figure out what we want. And I think we're pretty good at having the right discipline to think through whether a lot of other people are going to want it, too.”
- [He said] Starting over on the iPhone enclosure: “I came in one Monday morning, I said, 'I just don't love this. I can't convince myself to fall in love with this. And this is the most important product we've ever done.' And we pushed the reset button.”
- [He said] Halting production on Toy Story: “there came a time when we were forced to admit that the story wasn't great... We stopped production for five months” “it's so easy to fool yourself - to convince yourself that it is when you know in your heart that it isn't.”
- [He said] Apple TV: “We failed, so far... that's why I call it a hobby.” He described exactly what was wrong with the first version. It put iTunes content on the television: “it turns out that's not what people really wanted to do... That's frosting on the cake, but it's not the cake. What everybody really wanted, it turned out, was movies.”
- [He said] Choosing which waves to surf: “These waves of technology, you can see them way before they happen, and you just have to choose wisely which ones you're going to surf.” “we didn't want to get into any business where we didn't own or control the primary technology because you'll get your head handed to you.”
- [He said] Choosing retail locations: Fans would make a special drive, but Windows users would not. “we reduce that risk from a 20-minute drive to 20 footsteps”.
- [He said] Self-doubt before the iPod: “It makes you wonder sometimes whether you're wrong. Maybe our stuff isn't better, although we thought it was. Or maybe people don't care, which is even more depressing.”

### 2. Rechecking the transcript of The Lost Interview (1995, Bob Cringely)

Transcript: https://www.danielscrivner.com/articles/steve-jobs-the-lost-interview . Primary.
The lines cited in the earlier notes all check out. The follow-up adds three excerpts that show his reasoning:

- [He said] Process is not substance: “people get very confused that the process is the content. And that's, ultimately, the downfall of IBM... the best people are the ones that really understand the content, and they're a pain in the butt to manage. But you put up with it because they're so great at the content.”
- [He said] How to tell someone their work falls short: “to point out to them when their work isn't good enough. And to do it very clearly and to articulate why, and to get them back on track. And you need to do that in a way that does not call into question your confidence in their abilities”. Immediately afterward, he acknowledged: “the really good people have found it beneficial. Some people have hated it.”
- [He said] A good idea is not a good product (criticizing Sculley): He calls it a disease to think “a really great idea is 90% of the work”. A great deal of craft lies between an idea and a product, and the idea changes in the making. “Designing a product is keeping 5,000 things in your brain”.
- [He said] The gap between average and best: In most industries it is at most 2 to 1. In software it is 50 to 1 or even 100 to 1, which is why he put his energy into finding the best people.

### 3. Transcript of the original May 1998 iMac launch footage

https://allaboutstevejobs.com/videos/keynotes/imac_introduction_1998 . Primary.

- [He said] On the origin of the four-quadrant matrix, he said they went “back to business school 101” and asked what customers wanted: “what do our customers want in our products?... They wanted consumer products and they wanted pro products... they also wanted two different kinds of each one, desktop and portable.” “We've got to make four great products”.

### 4. October 2010 Top 100 agenda email

Made public in the 2014 Apple v. Samsung trial. Full text in Quartz: https://qz.com/196005/the-steve-jobs-email-that-outlined-apples-strategy-a-year-before-his-death . Primary (an internal email he wrote himself).

- [He wrote] “2011: Holy War with Google” “primary reason for this Top 100 meeting”; “tie all of our products together, so we further lock customers into our ecosystem”. He acknowledged “Google and Microsoft are further along on the technology” and that Apple was “in danger of hanging on to old paradigm too long”.
- Significance: In public he said Apple was not fixated on competitors and simply wanted to make good products. Internally the agenda led with war against Google and locking in users. Both were true.

### 5. Follow-up on individual events

- Scrapping the Apple Store prototype: Ron Johnson recounted it several times (Engadget 2014-07-17; Business Insider 2014-07). [Others said] Jobs initially replied, “Do you know how big a change that is? I don't have time to redesign the store”. Ten minutes later he entered the meeting room and said: “Well, Ron thinks our store is all wrong. And he's right, so I'm going to leave now. And Ron, you work with the team and design the store.” Corroborated secondary (firsthand recollection plus Isaacson).
- Top 100 whiteboard: Isaacson, “The Real Leadership Lessons of Steve Jobs,” HBR 2012-04. [Others said] On the last day each year he asked for “the ten things we should do next,” filled the board, crossed out what he thought was foolish, argued the list down to ten, then struck the last seven: “We can only do three.” Single secondary (the biographer).
- iDVD: Mike Evangelist's recollection, relayed by Isaacson. [Others said] After looking through a stack of interface mockups, he drew a rectangle on the whiteboard: “Here's the new application. It's got one window. You drag your video into the window. Then you click the button that says 'Burn.' That's it. That's what we're going to make.” Single secondary.
- The iPod's three-click rule: Isaacson. [Others said] He went through the interface screen by screen. Any song or function had to be reachable in three clicks, and it had to be obvious where to click. Single secondary.
- MobileMe: Adam Lashinsky, “How Apple Works: Inside the World's Biggest Startup,” Fortune 2011-05. [Others said] “Can anyone tell me what MobileMe is supposed to do?” “So why the fuck doesn't it do that?” He replaced the person in charge on the spot. A former employee offered another account (Business Insider 2013-04): Frontline staff had warned him early and asked for a phased rollout. He didn't listen, and “it was all his fault”. Both accounts stand side by side.
- Open letter on the iPhone price cut (2007-09-06): SFGate, CBC, and CNBC quoted the original that day. [He wrote] “we need to do a better job taking care of our early iPhone customers as we aggressively go after new ones with a lower price” “We apologize for disappointing some of you”. He maintained that cutting the price was right: “I am sure that we are making the correct decision”. Primary (open letter).
- Tim Cook on Jobs changing his mind (D10, 2012): “He would flip on something so fast that you would forget that he was the one taking the 180-degree polar opposite position the day before... it takes courage to say, 'I was wrong.'” Secondary (a colleague's account).
- Two versions of the Corning glass story: Isaacson records “Don't be afraid. You can do it.”. Wendell Weeks recalled it as “Do you know what your biggest problem is? You're afraid.” on a Fortune podcast in 2025. Both accounts come from the participants' side and should be shown in parallel, not merged. Isaacson also records the first half: Jobs started telling Weeks how to make glass. Weeks cut him off with “shut up and let me teach you some science”, and Jobs stopped talking and listened.
- Boot time (Andy Hertzfeld, “Saving Lives,” Folklore.org): The original says 50 million seconds a day: “Over a year, that's probably dozens of lifetimes. So if you make it boot ten seconds faster, you've saved a dozen lives.” Hertzfeld commented: “I'm not sure if this pitch had much effect, but we thought it was pretty humorous”.
- Pixar: Former CFO Lawrence Levy said that when he joined in 1994, Jobs “had put $50 million into Pixar and had nothing to show for it” (CNBC 2016-11-03). Fortune reported in 1995 that Jobs had considered selling Pixar to Microsoft (as relayed by Business Insider 2011-10). Corroborated secondary.

## III. How the findings differ from the prior assessment

| # | Finding | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | He was not someone who “never hedged” in speech. The transcripts contain plenty of I think, sort of, and you know, and his words about failure were plain (We failed, so far) | Overturned |
| 2 | “He never listened to users” does not hold. He traced the four quadrants to asking customers what they wanted. The line after “no market research” says that the first few hundred users were Apple itself. When something went wrong he read customers' emails, wrote an open letter, and offered compensation | Overturned |
| 3 | Pixar was not foresight plus patience. He misjudged the direction and tried unsuccessfully to sell it | Overturned |
| 4 | He reversed himself much faster than expected: four months on third-party apps, ten minutes on the store layout, one Monday morning to scrap the enclosure | Deepened |
| 5 | He had a recurring move he called “pushing the reset button.” Toy Story, the Apple Store, and the iPhone enclosure were stopped and restarted close to delivery. He discussed all three together | Added |
| 6 | The mechanics of the Monday review: An agenda stayed 80% the same and they went through all products in development and previous-week sales from the beginning each week. He called it one of Apple's few processes | Added |
| 7 | Delegation meant “letting them know everything,” not just their own area. He entrusted all operations to Cook: “I could just forget about a lot of things” | Deepened |
| 8 | He had a full argument against process (process is not substance, and confusing them brought down IBM), yet kept one particularly rigorous process himself | Deepened |
| 9 | His hiring question was “Why are you here?”. He looked beyond the answer itself. For seasoned hires competence was merely the ante. He wanted to know whether they'd fall in love with the company | Added |
| 10 | Public messaging differed from the internal agenda. In public: “We just want to make great products.” Internally, the first items were “holy war with Google” and locking in users | Added |
| 11 | After failures he acted on two tracks: internally he berated people and replaced leaders (MobileMe). Externally he conceded part and defended part (in the price-cut letter he apologized and compensated customers but defended the cut; during Antennagate he said Apple wasn't perfect but denied there was an Antennagate) | Deepened |
| 12 | Retelling amplified the reality distortion field. The boot-time participant said the pitch had little effect. At Corning, the other party first told him to stop talking and listen, and he did | Overturned |
| 13 | Widely repeated numbers such as “350 products cut to 10” and “the iPhone built in 18 months” lack sources | Overturned |
| 14 | His way of criticizing others' work had a requirement missing from his popular image: Do not make people doubt your confidence in their abilities. He also admitted that some people hated it | Deepened |
| 15 | Self-doubt is documented. Before the iPod, the Mac's market share was not rising: “It makes you wonder sometimes whether you're wrong” | Added |
| 16 | Earlier judgments that held and need no elaboration: focus means saying no, end-to-end control, technology and the humanities, A players, the three Stanford stories, and the major events of 1985 and 1997 | Confirmed |

Five overturned, five deepened, five added, and one cluster confirmed.

## IV. What the research did not find

- Apart from The Lost Interview, no verbatim reflection by Jobs on hardware failures in the 1985–1996 NeXT period turned up. Only biographical retellings were found.
- For the cancellation of the G4 Cube, the only account of Jobs's own view was Isaacson's retelling (overdesigned and overpriced).
- No statement from Jobs made at the time was found for the decision to replace iPod mini with nano in 2005.
- Full texts of Becoming Steve Jobs (Schlender & Tetzeli) and Creative Selection (Ken Kocienda) were not obtained. Only public excerpts were used. Kocienda's book contains many firsthand accounts of demo reviews and is the highest-priority lead for follow-up.
