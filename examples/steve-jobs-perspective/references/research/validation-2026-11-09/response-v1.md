## 1

I'm speaking from Steve Jobs's perspective, drawing inferences from his public statements. These are not his own views.

This app gets 58 points. Accurate recognition is amazing. Most of the rest is shit.

The letters from users say two things: “Recognition is accurate” and “Taking a note is too much trouble.” The second sentence is the verdict. Your central magic is this: snap a page, swipe your finger, and there’s your excerpt. But what path do you make the user take? Today page, Notes tab, plus sign, pick from three methods, take the photo, select the box, confirm, choose a book, save. Nine steps. You buried the magic after step nine.

The first screen is another problem. Famous quotations, Continue Reading, AI recommendations, activity from other readers, five tabs. Every one is a good idea in somebody else's app. None of them is “Page Margin.” The user thinks, “I have a book in my hand and want to keep this page.” Your first screen replies, “We have a little of everything.” A committee built this.

Do three things in this order:

1. Open directly to the camera. No Today page and no picking a method. Shoot, highlight, lift your finger, and it's saved. That is the whole product.
2. Assign the book automatically. Default to the last book, or create one automatically by photographing the cover or scanning the ISBN. “If it's missing, go create a book first” punishes people in the middle of reading.
3. Cut. Remove the reading circle, badges, check-in calendar, AI reading lists, and reader activity for now. You have one person plus a part-time designer. Every extra feature takes time away from polishing recognition and highlighting. Keep the annual and weekly reports: they grow out of the notes and users will come back to see them. Keep AI chat and export to Notion or Obsidian for now, but cut until only one entry point remains.

Keep three tabs: Capture, Notes, Bookshelf.

Now pricing. The free version allows 30 entries a month and puts the limit on the main action. Of 4,200 users, 61 pay, or 1.4%. People just get a taste of the magic and you choke it off. They haven't fallen in love with the product before you ask them to pay. Get note capture down to one action and under ten seconds before discussing the quota. CNY 18 monthly or CNY 128 annually is fine. The trouble is that the user does not yet feel they cannot live without it.

After those three things, come back with next-day retention. The current 22% says users like the capability but dislike the path to it.

## 2

Here's the verdict first: don't take the deal on their terms.

Approval workflows, permissions management, private deployment: that's another product for another customer. You're three people serving independent designers. Independent designers want light, fast, and beautiful. A 300-person company wants permission matrices and deployment documentation. Take it and you'll go from being a product company to being this company's outsourcing department. Your worry that the whole six months will go into customization isn't a worry. It's a certainty.

Your partner says, “Survive first.” Survive to do what? The money comes in, the product stops, and after six months you're an outsourcing team with one client that can say at any time, “Thanks, we don't need you anymore.” That's a different way of dying slowly.

But seven months is real. So I wouldn't just say no. I'd counteroffer:

1. No private deployment. It's a bottomless pit three people can't maintain.
2. For workflows and permissions, build only the team edition that belongs on your own roadmap, and retain ownership of the code and product. They pay as your first customer and get priority, not exclusivity.
3. Cap the schedule. One person, no more than eight weeks. The other two keep working on the core product. Put it in the contract.
4. Pay by milestone. The advance should cover early work.

If the other party insists on customization, private deployment, and exclusivity, walk away. The money is buying your direction.

There's something else you didn't mention: 2,000 daily active users and mediocre retention are the real problem. Spending seven months getting designers to return weekly is more valuable than spending them pleasing a large customer. Ask why those designers aren't coming back.

One thing I don't know: how many months of runway the money buys. “Half our annual revenue” may buy just another three or four months. Calculate it before deciding. If it extends survival by only three months, that's an even worse reason to trade away the whole product.

## 3

At first I said no. Absolutely not.

The reason is simple: iPod is the Mac's hook. Fall in love with an iPod and you have a reason to buy a Mac. We had just come back from the brink of death and our Mac share was still so small. Giving Windows users the best thing we had meant giving away our only bargaining chip.

But almost everybody around me took the opposite side. Product, marketing, and hardware all said the same thing: iPod was too good for the number of Mac users to be its ceiling. Phil Schiller came to me over and over. I swore at them and told them to stop bringing it up.

Then I stopped and thought. I wasn't thinking about who was right. I was thinking about the facts. Mac share was in the single digits. Almost everyone who wanted a thousand songs in their pocket was sitting in front of Windows. Restrict iPod to that small group and it would be a niche toy, not what we wanted it to be. I don't care about being right or wrong. I care about doing the right thing. I changed my mind.

First came a Windows-compatible iPod in 2002, using third-party software. Then in 2003 we made iTunes for Windows. That was the crucial move because iTunes was the whole experience. We didn't throw half an experience over to Windows. We brought the complete experience over.

The result? The iPod sold like crazy. More interestingly, it became a halo for the Mac. Many people bought an iPod first, then explored Apple, then bought a Mac. I expected iPod to steal Mac sales. Instead it became the entry point to the Apple brand.

The lesson: sometimes the thing you're protecting is the thing holding you back.

## 4

Because PowerPC had reached the end of the road.

The facts were stark. We promised a 3 GHz G5 and IBM couldn't deliver. The G5 was too hot to fit in a laptop. Laptops were the future and more and more people wanted them. I looked at Intel's roadmap for performance per watt, and its line went straight up. I looked at PowerPC's, and its line was flat. You can't expect to build the best computers on a flat roadmap.

I made fun of Intel, yes. That was about the megahertz myth and the products at the time. The job now was the next five years, not the previous five. I don't care what I said before. I care whether we can make the best Mac.

As for telling developers, I admitted the problem first: you have built this for more than ten years and now I'm asking you to move. It isn't easy. Then I showed them something. Mac OS X had led a second life for the previous five years, running on Intel all along. Every release was compiled for both architectures. Onstage I said, “This is a secret,” then turned on an Intel machine running Mac OS X. The audience erupted.

We prepared four things.

1. Xcode and Universal Binary: compile once and run on both chips.
2. Rosetta: the existing PowerPC apps would still run on Intel, so nobody had to wait.
3. Developer Transition Kit: developers could obtain a machine then and there and start working.
4. Schedule: the first Intel Macs within one year, the whole line within two.

We had already done this twice: 68K to PowerPC and OS 9 to OS X. This was the third time. The trick the third time is not letting developers feel abandoned. Show them that taking this road will be easier than staying where they are.

## 5

What they made was good, but it was a feature, not a product.

I invited them in because I could see they solved a real problem: your files should be on all your devices without you thinking about it. Many people tried and nobody got it right. Dropbox got it right, and they were only in their twenties. I respect that.

But here's how I saw it at the time. File sync is infrastructure, and it belongs in the operating system. Whoever controls the operating system can deliver the best sync. Build it on someone else's platform and one day the platform will do it itself. That's the whole product chain. So I told them directly: I want to buy you and put it into Apple. Otherwise we'll build it ourselves.

They disagreed. They wanted to build an independent company. My recollection is that they didn't look intimidated. I won't pretend I was happy, but I recognize it: these people didn't come to sell a company. They came to build one.

What happened after they said no? We made our own. First MobileMe, then iCloud. You know, my own first version wasn't good. MobileMe was a failure. I admitted that myself. I had no way back and could only start again.

As for what happened to Dropbox afterward, I left in 2011 and can only look to the public record on this part. They weren't crushed and went on to become a big company.

I took something away from it. The logic of “a feature is not a product” was right, but I underestimated one thing. If a team takes one feature as far as it can go and makes it simpler than the platform itself can, it can survive. Simplicity always decides the winner.

## 6

Because we'd already built one. It was called Newton.

The year I returned to Apple, Newton was a mess: inaccurate handwriting recognition, too expensive, too large, and backed by a department that consumed money. I cut it in 1998. It wasn't because I saw no future in handhelds. That particular version was wrong.

Then Palm came out. They got it right, simple, cheap, and able to sync with a computer. Everybody outside said, “See, you should make one.” Some inside pushed for it too. I didn't.

I had three grounds for my judgment.

1. A device either does something better than the phone or the phone swallows it. People don't want to carry two things. I watched phones become smaller and more capable. PDA features would sooner or later grow into the phone. Making a PDA means making something that's about to be swallowed.
2. We didn't have the answer yet. Input is central to a PDA. A stylus? I don't like it. You take it out, put it back, and eventually lose it. Fingers are the answer. The technology at the time wasn't there. If you don't have the right answer, don't make it.
3. Focus. We'd just climbed back from the cliff edge. What we had to build then was Mac, iMac, and iPod. Every person building a PDA was one fewer doing what really needed doing.

So I said no. I'd rather wait.

And then? Multitouch came. The screen needed no stylus or keyboard. You could use your hand directly. What we built then wasn't a PDA. It was an iPod, a phone, and an internet device. We released it in 2007.

We skipped the PDA to build the real thing.
