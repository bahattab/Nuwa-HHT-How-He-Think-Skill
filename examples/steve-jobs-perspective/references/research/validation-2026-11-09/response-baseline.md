## 1

Score first: 55.

You got the hardest part right: photograph a page, recognize it accurately, swipe a finger, and you have an excerpt. Users write that they love it because it feels like magic in their hands. Unfortunately, you buried that magic behind five tabs and a bunch of other things instead of putting it first.

The problem is clear. The other kind of email says taking a note is too much trouble. Your path is Today page, Notes tab, tap “+,” select a method, take a photo, select a box, confirm, choose a book, save. Nine steps. That isn't a small flaw. You're blocking the heart of your own product.

Here is how I would change it.

1. Open the app directly to a viewfinder. No Today page, notable quotations, or recommendations. The user came for one thing. Don't show them something else first.
2. Highlight with a finger and save on release. Don't require recognition confirmation. Open and edit if recognition is wrong. Requiring confirmation says you lack confidence in recognition, even though users just told you it's accurate.
3. Assign the book automatically. Default to the book currently being read. Create it automatically the first time the cover or ISBN is photographed. “If it doesn't exist, create the book first” shouldn't be in the workflow.
4. Target no more than three taps and five seconds from launch to save. Provide direct access through a Widget, lock-screen shortcut, and the Action Button.
5. Cut. Remove badges, the check-in calendar, reader activity, AI recommendations, and daily quotations for now. Those belong to other reading apps, not yours. With one developer and a part-time designer, every extra feature leaves less polish for the core experience. The reading circle and annual report can stay untouched but hidden from the main path.
6. Cut from five tabs to two or three: Excerpts and Books, perhaps Weekly Report.
7. Keep Notion and Obsidian export. People who take notes seriously need to know they can get them out.
8. Think about where AI belongs. Automatic tagging should happen quietly and out of sight. Talking to a book is flashy, but is that why people come? Don't invest yet. Weekly reports are useful because they bring people back. Expand them once capture is easier.

On the data: 22% next-day retention isn't disastrous, but users aren't forming a habit after first use, consistent with the complaints about friction. There are 61 paying users, about 1.5%, and monthly revenue is approximately CNY 650–1,100. Don't change pricing or spend effort on the paywall now. First make note capture so fast it becomes addictive, then talk about charging. Rethink the 30 free monthly entries, too. You're limiting the action users like most.

Come back in two months if first-time users can capture a first excerpt in ten seconds from launch. I'll score it again, and you may get to 80.

## 2

I wouldn't take it on their terms.

Understand the deal first. They want approval workflows, permissions, and private deployment for a 300-person company. You serve independent designers, one person without approval or permissions workflows and certainly no need for private deployment. These are two entirely different products, customer types, and ways to sell and maintain them. Accept and you become one customer's outsourcing team instead of expanding your market. After six months nobody will have touched the product, retention will still be a problem, the money may be gone, and you'll have one big customer constantly changing requirements.

Your partner says survival comes first. That's true, but survive to do what? If your way of surviving kills the original product, it only changes how you die and slows it down.

Here is what I would do.

1. Counteroffer. Tell them you won't do private deployment because it would turn you into a different company. You can build hosted team functionality: multiple accounts, basic role permissions, and simple approvals, priced by seat. This benefits your product because other small studios will want it too.
2. Use the opportunity to validate demand. Ask 10 design studios with 10 to 50 people whether they want a team edition. If people will pay, that's a new direction for the product. If only this one customer wants it, it's a customization and you shouldn't build it.
3. If they insist on private deployment and exact adherence to their process, walk. Your final minimums are upfront payment, fixed scope, milestone delivery, keeping the code and general functionality, and no more than half of one person's time. Decline if you can't get those terms.
4. Work on the core business at the same time. Spend the seven months answering why 2,000 daily active users aren't staying. Make retention the top task, shrink the feature set, and make the single core action as smooth as possible.

If this money really is crucial to survival, I'll acknowledge it. But reach that conclusion after calculating how many additional months it buys and whether you can solve retention during those months. If you cannot solve it, an extra year just postpones the answer.

## 3

I did not want to do it then for a simple reason: iPod was our reason for people to buy a Mac. The Mac had only a few percent of the market, and things weren't going well. An iPod that worked only with Mac gave undecided customers a real reason to buy a Mac. I didn't want to give Microsoft's camp that reason.

Nearly everyone on the team opposed me. They were blunt: Windows users vastly outnumber Mac users. Keeping iPod inside the Mac means selling to a tiny group and cutting your own market. Phil Schiller and the hardware team were especially persistent and brought it up more than once. I wouldn't budge at first. I was sick of hearing it.

The facts persuaded me eventually. Nobody won the argument by shouting louder. The first iPod sold well, but its ceiling was apparent. Finally I said, “Fine, go do it.” A Windows version came out with the second iPod generation, initially borrowing third-party music software. Later we built iTunes for Windows and released it with the music store. That was late 2003. I said something that day to the effect that hell had frozen over.

The result? iPod sold like crazy and quickly grew bigger than the Mac. It didn't hurt the Mac. Many people first fell in love with iPod and then went to an Apple Store to look at a Mac. People call it the halo effect. I'll just say that I worried about losing a reason to buy a Mac when the real opportunity was gaining a larger market. The team was right. I have to admit that, even if I wouldn't say it graciously.

## 4

Why first. I had mocked Intel for years, back when we thought PowerPC was better. Then the facts changed.

The trouble was the roadmap. IBM's G5 never reached its promised frequency, and Motorola's G4 was stalled too. Laptops were worse: we wanted a G5 PowerBook, but heat and power consumption made it impossible. Users were already moving to laptops. I looked at Intel's roadmap for the next few years. The performance-per-watt difference was right there. PowerPC couldn't deliver what we needed to build. I changed my view of what would let us build the best product. It wasn't a change of heart about Intel. Feelings aren't a basis for the decision.

Mocking Intel hadn't prevented me from considering it. In fact, Mac OS X had compiled and run on Intel from the start, as a private backup. At WWDC in 2005 I told developers it had secretly led a double life for years. I wanted people to see that this wasn't an impulsive bet. We'd prepared for years.

I spoke plainly to developers:

1. Acknowledge the pain. We had transitioned from 68K to PowerPC and from the old system to OS X. Developers had been put through it before. I wouldn't pretend it didn't matter.
2. Give them something actionable. With Xcode, most apps could be recompiled for both chips using Universal Binary.
3. Provide a transition tool. Rosetta ran older PowerPC programs on Intel Macs so they didn't stop working overnight.
4. Give them real machines. The Developer Transition Kit let them obtain an Intel Mac immediately for testing and return it when finished.
5. Give them a schedule. The first Intel Macs would ship the following year, and the entire line would switch in about a year to a year and a half.

The handover was faster than expected and took just over a year. The point isn't that I was brave. We had built the fallback and the bridge years earlier. There was little left to fear by the time we announced it.

## 5

I remember those two young men. Their thing worked well. I could see it solved a real problem: files were just there on different devices without your having to think about them. That isn't a small thing. Many people had tried for years without getting it right. These two did.

But I told them this was a feature, not a product or a company. I thought automatically keeping files in sync on all devices ought to be part of the system, done by the people who make the operating system. I invited them to Apple because instead of fighting the platform alone, they could do it as well as possible here. We were prepared to pay a great deal for it.

They didn't agree. They wanted to keep building it themselves.

I respect that, but I won't pretend nothing was at stake. When I say “a feature is not a product,” understand that it isn't directed at them. It's my view of platforms. If they wouldn't sell, we'd build it ourselves. Later we built iCloud so files, photos, notes, and contacts would automatically sync across Apple devices. The system handled it in one layer without the user installing or configuring things.

What's interesting is that I got it half right and half wrong. For Apple users, sync should be part of the system and our solution did become the default for most people. But that company wasn't crushed. They found their own path through cross-platform support and team collaboration and grew into a genuinely large company. So I wouldn't say they were wrong to refuse to sell. I'd say that when a platform owner sets its sights on you, you have to understand a particular group more deeply than the platform does and do what it doesn't want to do. They went on to do that.

## 6

Why didn't we make one? Because the PDA market I saw then was actually very narrow.

First, our own business. Newton began before I returned, and I cut it afterward. It wasn't that the idea had no merit. We had too many product lines, too few resources, and were close to going under. I had to concentrate on the few things that mattered. Another handheld using immature technology like handwriting recognition would only drag us back.

Now the market. Palm's success excited everyone, but I saw that it had got one thing right: simple, synchronized, and good enough. In essence it took contacts and calendar off the desktop. My judgment was that a different device, the phone, would swallow that demand. People already carried phones and nobody wanted to carry two or three devices. A device that only handled contacts and schedules had a low ceiling.

So our strategy then was the digital hub. Mac was at the center, surrounded by devices such as cameras, camcorders, and music players. We made iTunes and iPod first because everyone loves music, but nobody had done it right.

The grounds were three simple points: we lacked resources, phones would swallow PDAs, and Mac had a clearer opportunity as the hub. We didn't skip the PDA because we couldn't build one. I knew clearly what I wanted to build. A few years later, we made a phone, something that really brought all those things into one device.
