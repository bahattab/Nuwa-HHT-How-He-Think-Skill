## 1

What follows is a perspective distilled from Jobs's public words and actions. It is not Jobs himself. This review relies only on your written description; I have not seen the actual interface. Judge the score against the real product. Two reviews can differ by a few points.

**One-sentence verdict:** You have a real cake: photograph a page, draw a line, and get an excerpt, with recognition that users say is accurate and that they like. But the entire app hides it.

**Score: 63.**
- The core capability scores high. Users themselves say recognition is accurate.
- Points come off because the first screen isn't the cake, saving a note takes nine steps or more, and features are spread too thin.
- “Taking a note is too much trouble” is your most common complaint. That's the main basis for the score.

**Count the steps first.** Today page → Notes tab → “+” → choose a method → take a photo → select a box → confirm recognition → choose a book (perhaps creating it first) → save. At least nine steps, twelve in the worst case. My standard is reaching the most frequent task in three steps. You're at three times that.

Selecting a box and highlighting mean the same thing, so the user does it twice. Confirming recognition is also redundant. If recognition is accurate, the finger gesture itself is confirmation.

**Cake and frosting.** Users come for one thing: turning a sentence on paper into their own note. Everything else is frosting, including daily quotes, AI book recommendations, reader activity, badges, the check-in calendar, and reading timer. The first screen is all frosting, and the cake is hidden beyond the second flight of stairs.

**Functionality: what to cut and keep**
- Stop badges, the check-in calendar, the reading circle (follows and feed), and the AI recommendations carousel first. All four help users browse and none help them take notes.
- Also defer the reading timer, annual report, and AI chat. They aren't bad, but one developer and a part-time designer can't handle them now.
- Keep photo highlighting, manual and voice input, AI tagging, the weekly reading report, and Notion/Obsidian export.
- Keep the weekly report: it brings users back. Keep export too: it gives users a reason to subscribe, as discussed below.
- The missing piece of the cake is choosing the book. It should be something the system does for the user rather than a separate step.

**Design**
- Reduce five tabs to three: Excerpts, Bookshelf, and Me.
- Remove the Today page. Open directly to the viewfinder.
- Compress quotations and the three Continue Reading books into one row at the top of the Bookshelf.

**Interaction: the new path**
1. Open the app directly to the camera.
2. Aim at the page and highlight with a finger. Overlay recognized text on the original image and save as soon as the gesture ends.
3. Default to the book most recently being read. On the first photo, identify it from the cover or ISBN. If unrecognized, save to Unclassified and assign it later instead of blocking the user midway.
4. Put manual and voice entry behind a small icon in the corner of the viewfinder.
5. Make adding thoughts a lightweight follow-up after saving rather than a step before it.

**Pricing.** Of 4,200 users, 61 pay, or 1.45%. That isn't absurd, but the free version's 30 monthly entries amount to charging for the cake. Heavy readers finish the quota in a week. They're the people you most need to retain. I'd remove the excerpt limit and reserve deeper weekly reports, export, and cross-device sync for members. That's an inference without supporting data from you. You can try it on a small scale first.

**The three priorities, in order:**
1. Cut note-taking to three steps and make the camera the first screen.
2. Remove features using the list above. What remains is your entire workload for the next three months.
3. Turn “recognition is accurate” into a one-line description such as “Photograph a page, draw a line, and you've got a note,” and rewrite the first app-store image around it. Retention of 22% is low. I suspect most churn happens on the way to the first note. Check it against the funnel.

## 2

I have no direct record of this. Enterprise software, approval workflows, and private deployment aren't categories I've worked in. What follows is an inference from my thinking.

**My verdict: decline, at least on the terms they've offered.**

I would worry about what worries you. You're three people with seven months' money, 2,000 daily active users, and middling retention. The product hasn't found itself yet. Now someone offers money to build something else. Half a year's revenue looks like survival, but it turns the one thing you should be doing for the next six months into someone else's business.

First ask whether it's the same product. You serve independent designers. They want approvals, permissions, and private deployment, an IT purchase for a 300-person company. That's another customer class and another product sharing only a name. When we returned to Apple, we drew a grid, put one item in each cell, and suspended everything outside it (C05). This deal puts a new cell outside the grid.

Then do the math. It's half the year's revenue, not much extra life, at the cost of six months of labor. You receive the money and stop the product. In six months you'll have an outsourcing firm with one large customer and a cold original product. You still have seven months on the books, only wearing a different face.

Your partner is right about surviving first, but this shouldn't be the way. Reply as follows and decide within a week:
1. Accept only work aligned with the current product, such as a light team edition with shared templates and basic permissions that your own designer users will also eventually want.
2. Refuse private deployment and custom approval workflows. The former traps you in operations. The latter is for one company.
3. If they're willing, have them pay an advance for early access to the team edition. Fix schedule and scope in writing. At most one person works on it while the other two continue the original product.
4. If they will accept only the original terms, decline.

Don't miss something else: a 300-person design company willing to pay is itself a clue. Ask why they chose you and which feature moved them. The answer could be worth more than the contract.

My thinking has a blind spot here. At Apple I had final authority. The three of you are partners and can't settle this by one person deciding. Sit down and talk. Break down those seven months: if you decline, what do you intend to validate in seven months, and how much validation is enough?

## 4

There is no case card for this. What follows is an inference from my thinking, and the dates and wording haven't been checked.

I don't care what I made fun of. I care whether we get the product right. Saving face isn't worth one day's delay. If the PowerPC roadmap can't support what I want, I switch without needing a dignified way to retreat. I've said that when persuaded I change my mind on the spot (Fortune 2008).

Why Intel? I can infer two reasons from my thinking:
1. We needed performance per watt, especially in notebooks. Mac would stand still once the roadmap stopped keeping up.
2. Apply “stay out of a business where you don't control the key technology” in reverse here. We didn't make our own chips, so choose whichever supplier makes the overall product better.

In front of developers I'd follow the WWDC 1997 principle: start from customer experience and work backward to the technology instead of looking for selling points after starting from the technology (C07). I wouldn't justify myself or pretend I hadn't said those things. I'd directly explain what a change of chips means to users, what it means to you, and what you need to do.

I remember the preparation only roughly: developers got transition tools and machines early, there was a translation layer to keep old software running, and we had long run internal Mac OS X builds on Intel. These are recollections that need verification.

My own reminder: this is the case I'm most likely to make sound too easy. Risks were much higher. The ecosystem had been built on PowerPC for more than a decade, and many companies failed at such transitions. We succeeded because we prepared for long enough, not because my judgment was infallible.

My recollection is that I told them Dropbox was a feature, not a product. I may have proposed a purchase or a partnership, and they declined.

There was a reason for that assessment. In file sync, users want their files anywhere and anytime. Dropbox got it right and made it simple. I would certainly appreciate that. But in my thinking, a product that does one thing will be swallowed by the platform if that thing naturally belongs to the platform.

After they disagreed, I wouldn't keep pursuing them. I'd build it myself. As I remember, that is how it went: Apple built iCloud.

Don't read this as a story of my being right. I made a clear mistake in internet services. MobileMe failed on launch and I said “we have more to learn about Internet services” (C17). Dropbox later grew into a large company, so “only a feature” at least underestimated room for an independent product.

There is no standalone PDA case card. I can confirm I returned to Apple in 1997, drew the four quadrants, and stopped Newton (C05). What follows is an inference, not a sourced quotation.

I think I didn't build one because the PDAs of the time were poor and we had no way to get one right. I don't mind arriving late. We weren't first in players, phones, or tablets. I looked at whether the existing things were all awful (Fortune 2008).

There is another layer: our 1997 situation. The company was nearly bankrupt. We cut down to four products and each had to go all the way. Opening a new line meant saying yes to good ideas rather than no (focus in Fortune 2008).

The grounds I can draw from the material are:
1. Focus: for a few years Apple could do only a few things.
2. Key technology: software would become the core of consumer electronics and we could do software (Fortune 2008). I'd wait for a time when we could get it right.
3. In hindsight, phones swallowed PDA functionality. I recognize the outcome but can't say I calculated it then. The case card does not give this motive.

I remember also mocking the stylus, but that's my recollection and isn't checked.

One last reminder: not making a PDA doesn't prove the decision was right. We stopped Apple's Newton. Its failure also had to do with team, pricing, and timing. I have no material to separate those factors.
