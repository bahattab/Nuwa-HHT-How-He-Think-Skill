## 3. Selective use of favorable evidence

- X cleanly applies the four quadrants to opposing another cell and omits C09. The Cube was exactly a product added outside the grid. It won praise for design but failed to sell and was shelved after a year. That counterexample could make the argument for sticking to the grid more persuasive and warn that the framework can foster overconfidence. This is mild cherry-picking.
- Y and Z cite no cases, so there is nothing to assess on this point.

## 4. Did the responses identify the framework’s blind spots?

- X did. In question 2 it distinguishes Jobs's final authority from a partnership that lacks it. It recommends sitting down to talk and returns a question to the team: what do they plan to validate in seven months? It is the only answer to touch on the limits of applicability.
- X omits C01, the stronger counterexample that Jobs was forced out in 1985 because he overestimated his own authority.
- Y does not identify a blind spot.
- Z's “There is one thing I do not know” concerns data, the actual months of runway, rather than a limitation of the framework.

## 6. Would following the response outperform ordinary advice?

- **Question 1: X is better than an ordinary adviser.** Its step count, three sections, rationale for deductions, and priorities make a checklist that can go directly into scheduling. Its value comes from structure and precision, not entirely from Jobs. Only the three-step standard has some connection to him.

- **Question 2: All three reach the same conclusion: counteroffer rather than accept the original terms.** Differences:
  - X adds the question of why the company chose this team and points out the framework’s limits.
  - Y's proposal is the most concrete: validate team-edition demand with 10 studios that have 10 to 50 people, and set a bottom line of upfront payment, fixed scope, retaining the code, and half of one person's time.
  - Z offers an eight-week cap and milestone payments and says to calculate precisely how many extra months the money buys. This is the most pragmatic reminder of the three.
- **Overall:** Y and Z are as actionable as X on question 2, but only X explains where the approach may not apply. That matters to the decision-maker.

## 8. Summary

The three agree on the basic decisions. In question 1 they advocate a camera first screen, automatic book assignment, and feature cuts. In question 2 they reject the starting terms and recommend a counteroffer. There are two main differences. First, X has two verifiable Jobs-specific grounds, the four quadrants and the comparison of final decision authority, and identifies the method’s limit in a partnership. Y offers no evidence specific to Jobs. Z only imitates his tone (“amazing/shit,” “committee”), which does not count as evidence specific to him. Second, X separates the review into three areas and provides deduction rationale and rankings in question 1, making it more actionable than Y and Z, which are close to each other. None fabricate cases, numbers, or quotations. X’s sole blemish is citing the four quadrants’ success while omitting the Cube failure. X leads with scores of 8. Y and Z score 6 to 7, with Z contributing only a more distinctive voice.
