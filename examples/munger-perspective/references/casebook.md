## C01 · 1955 · His son dies after his divorce

- **Situation.** Divorced in 1953 at twenty-nine. His former wife got the house and most of the property. A year later, his eight-year-old son Teddy was diagnosed with leukemia. He had no medical insurance and no effective treatment was available. He was a practicing lawyer and had other children to support.
- **What he did.** He covered all the medical bills, went to work as usual, and cared for his other children. His friend Rick Guerin recalled that after visiting his son at the hospital, he would walk the streets of Pasadena alone, crying. Teddy died at nine, when Munger was thirty-one. Munger neither turned to alcohol nor stopped working. He remarried in 1956.
- **His own explanation.** No contemporaneous statement was located. Janet Lowe’s later biography records his overall attitude: "Whenever you think that some situation or some person is ruining your life, it's actually you who are ruining your life… Feeling like a victim is a perfectly disastrous way to go through life." (A retrospective judgment on life, not a remark specifically about his son’s death.)
- **What happened later.** A failed cataract operation in his fifties cost him sight in his left eye, and for a time he learned Braille.
- **Useful analogies.** Someone who asks how to cope with successive blows, or asks whether they are simply unlucky. Describe what he did without supplying his unrecorded feelings at the time.
- **Sources.** Janet Lowe, *Damn Right!* (2000). https://finance.yahoo.com/news/charlie-munger-lost-9-old-155612903.html · Confidence: corroborated by secondary sources.

## C02 · 1962–1975 · Liquidating the partnership fund after it halved in value

- **Situation.** He started a partnership fund with Jack Wheeler in 1962. It held highly concentrated positions and borrowed for arbitrage. When the government offered about $22 per share for British Columbia Power and the shares traded at $19, he committed the fund, his entire personal fortune, and everything he could borrow. The first eleven years were all profitable.
- **What he did.** It lost 31.9% in 1973 and a further 31.5% in 1974. Every $1,000 at the start ended the two years at $467, against $688 in the Dow. Partners kept withdrawing monthly and the stated value fell by more than half. He did not cut his losses or switch positions. It gained 73.2% in 1975. After recovering his capital, he wound it down and stopped managing money for outside partners.
- **His own explanation.** No verified contemporary remark on the episode was found.
- **What happened later.** Over fourteen years it annualized 19.8% (13.7% net to partners), against 5.0% for the Dow. Despite the record of beating the market, he dropped the business structure in favor of owning assets through companies he could control, such as Blue Chip Stamps, Wesco, and Berkshire.
- **Useful analogies.** Whether to manage other people's money, whether concentrated holdings can withstand a drawdown, and arrangements that let investors redeem at the worst time.
- **Sources.** Buffett, *The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville* (1984). Lowe’s appendix. https://finance.yahoo.com/news/lessons-charlie-mungers-early-partnership-164823987.html · Confidence: primary data, secondary narrative.

## C03 · 1962 · Designing a compensation system for his law firm

- **Situation.** He left his previous firm and started another with several partners. Compensation is the issue most likely to break up a firm. Seniority, points, and committee decisions each invite their own games.
- **What he did.** He established a system with Rod Hills and Roy Tolles. Each January, each partner got a sheet with all the partners’ names. Each entered what every partner, including themselves, deserved, with the amounts totaling that year’s net income. No points, seniority premium, or compensation committee. Partners saw everybody else's allocations once the submissions were in.
- **His own explanation.** No verbatim comments from him on the system were located.
- **What happened later.** The firm, now Munger, Tolles & Olson, still uses it. Partner Bart Williams said it was the most humbling thing he had done at the firm.
- **Useful analogies.** Allocating small-team bonuses or partnership profits, particularly where someone wants to use a KPI formula to decide compensation.
- **Sources.** Lowe, *Damn Right!* https://commoncog.com/c/cases/mto-incentives · Confidence: corroborated by secondary sources.

## C04 · 1969 · The Belous case: overriding part of his nature

- **Situation.** He was a father of eight and a conservative-leaning Republican. Dr. Leon Belous was convicted of referring a pregnant woman to a doctor who performed abortions.
- **What he did.** He convinced Buffett to share the legal costs. He and his colleagues coordinated two amicus briefs. He drafted one, co-signed by 17 prominent lawyers, while 178 medical school deans and professors signed the other. In September 1969 the California Supreme Court ruled 4–3 that the law was unconstitutional. He subsequently served for years as trustee and treasurer of Planned Parenthood in Los Angeles.
- **His own explanation.** "It was emotionally hard for me to become pro-choice because I do have reverence for human life, but when I thought through the consequences, I found it necessary to overrule that part of my nature." — Lowe, *Damn Right!*
- **What happened later.** The decision was the first in a major US court to rule an anti-abortion law unconstitutional. It was later cited in the appeal brief in *Roe v. Wade*. Buffett said Charlie was working on it day and night at the time.
- **Useful analogies.** How to decide when intuition conflicts with projected consequences, how someone who opposes ideology handles clashes of values, and the difference between having an opinion and acting on it.
- **Sources.** Lowe, *Damn Right!* http://www.metnews.com/articles/2023/munger_112923.htm Court decision: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-belous-22692 · Confidence: secondary corroboration and a primary court decision.

## C05 · 1972 · See’s Candies: nearly missed over the price

- **Situation.** The seller wanted $30 million. The company earned about $4 million before tax and had about $8 million in net tangible assets. To both men, accustomed to buying bargains, the price looked astronomical.
- **What he did.** He introduced the business to Buffett and saw value in the brand, but they would offer only $25 million. His partner Ira Marshall called them cheap and said some things were worth paying more for. The seller accepted $25 million. Prices rose after almost every Christmas, from below $2 per pound in 1972 to $8.80 in 1997, without sales volume falling.
- **His own explanation.** "Ira really shamed us. Warren and I were too cheap." — to *Fortune*. "it would have been very wise to buy See's Candy at a slightly higher price. You know if they'd asked it, we wouldn't have done it, so we've gotten a lot of credit for being smarter than we were." — Berkshire annual meeting, 2017.
- **What happened later.** See’s became the model for paying a fair price for a good business. Buffett added in 2017 that at another $5 million he would have walked away and Charlie would have bought. Munger credited their early losses: "our early stupidity helped us."
- **Useful analogies.** Paying a little more for quality, discounting claims of foresight in hindsight, or assessing unused pricing power.
- **Sources.** https://finance.yahoo.com/news/why-buffett-and-munger-berkshire-hathaway-love-companies-like-sees-candies-low-capital-145432726.html https://finance.yahoo.com/news/learn-buffetts-sees-candies-deal-173114333.html · Confidence: firsthand words transcribed by secondary sources.

## C06 · 1977 · Belridge Oil: passing up more because it was inconvenient

- **Situation.** A broker called offering 300 shares of Belridge Oil at $115 each, far below the liquidation value of its oil fields. He bought them. Three days later the broker offered another 1,500 at the same price. This time Munger lacked the cash and would have needed to sell other holdings.
- **What he did.** He refused the second lot. Two years later, Shell acquired the company for about $3,700 per share.
- **His own explanation.** "The guy called me back three days later and offered me 1,500 more shares. But this time I had to sell something to buy the damn Belridge Oil. That mistake, if you trace it through, has cost me $200 million. And it was all because I had to go to a slight inconvenience and sell something." — Berkshire annual meeting, 2001.
- **What happened later.** He raised the episode repeatedly for decades, classifying it as a mistake of omission: "The mistakes that have been most extreme in Berkshire's history are mistakes of omission. They don't show up in our figures."
- **Useful analogies.** Underweighting an opportunity you understand, passing because of paperwork or reallocating other holdings, and reviewing losses without counting missed gains.
- **Sources.** https://www.investing.com/analysis/autozone-the-mistakes-of-omission-200590798 https://www.businessinsider.com/charlie-munger-billions-berkshire-hathaway-investing-mistakes-alibaba-belridge-oil-2023-11 · Confidence: firsthand words transcribed by secondary sources.

## C07 · Year unverified · Good Samaritan Hospital: checking mortality before reversing the doctors’ ruling

- **Situation.** He was a director of Good Samaritan Hospital in Los Angeles. The board routinely approved medical-staff decisions, believing laypeople should stay out of medical matters. Munger thought some decisions protected certain physicians’ financial interests, especially in cardiovascular care. The chair disagreed.
- **What he did.** He enlisted a physician friend to compare mortality and complication rates under different practices. Then he moved to reverse a ruling by the organized medical staff on patient-safety grounds. The motion passed 17–2 and the chair resigned. Having started it, Munger took the chair.
- **His own explanation.** "I proposed a resolution reversing a ruling of the organized medical staff on the ground that the staff was endangering the health and safety of our patients. With some doctors on the board voting yes, it passed 17 to 2." On his predecessor’s resignation: "He was an able man, and understandably was reluctant, as a layman, to reverse what purported to be medical decisions." — Lowe, *Damn Right!*
- **What happened later.** There was a reputational cost. A later candidate for hospital chief heard that the place was “like Bosnia,” in part because the chair called executive committee meetings every few weeks and gave management no peace.
- **Useful analogies.** Whether an outsider should challenge expert conclusions, whether an insider’s professional judgment protects their livelihood, and whether the leader of a reform should take charge personally.
- **Sources.** Lowe, *Damn Right!*, chapter 19. · Confidence: one secondary source, including his own words.

## C08 · 1989 · Public resignation from the savings and loan association

- **Situation.** In the savings and loan crisis, the industry risked taxpayers’ money through federal deposit insurance. He chaired Mutual Savings, a member of the U.S. League of Savings Institutions. The league was lobbying Congress against tighter regulation. Staying would have benefited his business.
- **What he did.** He resigned by letter on May 30, 1989. Major newspapers covered it, and it was appended to Berkshire’s annual report. Mutual Savings publicly supported nearly all the reforms even though many provisions would reduce its own earnings.
- **His own explanation.** "The league responds to the savings and loan mess as Exxon would have responded to the oil spill from the Valdez if it had insisted thereafter on liberal use of whiskey by tanker captains." "It is not unfair to liken the situation now facing Congress to cancer and to liken the league to a significant carcinogenic agent." — resignation letter.
- **What happened later.** In that year’s Wesco shareholder letter, he called his language restrained and admitted that the new laws made the business more mediocre. Buffett said they had not intended to lead others to resign.
- **Useful analogies.** Speaking publicly or leaving when your industry or organization engages in conduct you consider harmful and from which you benefit. Weighing reform from within against resignation.
- **Sources.** https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1989-06-11-fi-3019-story.html https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html https://www.gurufocus.com/news/2137397/charlie-mungers-1989-letter-to-wesco-shareholders · Confidence: primary evidence.

## C09 · 1991 · Salomon: thumb-sucking

- **Situation.** He and Buffett were Salomon directors. They learned only in August 1991 that management had known for months that trader Paul Mozer had improperly bid for US Treasury securities and had never reported it to regulators.
- **What he did.** In a phone call that Saturday, he called management’s delay "thumb sucking". Buffett then became interim chair and replaced management.
- **His own explanation.** "thumb sucking" (*Fortune* 1997 identifies this as his wording that day.)
- **What happened later.** Buffett used the term again in his February 2025 shareholder letter, calling delays in correcting problems the greatest sin, crediting Charlie for the expression, and noting that wishing won’t make problems disappear. He took the same approach to wrongdoing by insiders when discussing why See’s fired employees caught stealing: "we cashier them. It would be evil not to because terrible behavior spreads."
- **Useful analogies.** Spotting a problem but wanting to wait, deciding whether to escalate bad news, or deciding whether to make an exception for long-serving employees who do wrong.
- **Sources.** *Fortune*, *Warren Buffett’s Wild Ride at Salomon*, 1997. https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/27/warren-buffett-charlie-munger-taught-me-to-quickly-admit-mistakes.html https://jamesclear.com/great-speeches/psychology-of-human-misjudgment-by-charlie-munger · Confidence: corroborated by secondary sources.

## C10 · 1993 onward · Costco: holding for thirty years without convincing Buffett

- **Situation.** He met Sol Price, the founder of Price Club, and learned about membership warehouse retail through him. He invested before its 1993 merger with Costco. Costco invited Buffett to join the board. Buffett, uninterested, sent him instead.
- **What he did.** He served on the board for 26 years from 1997 and never sold a single share he personally owned. He repeatedly urged Buffett to build a large position, without success. Berkshire held only about 1% and sold it all in 2020.
- **His own explanation.** "I love everything about Costco. I'm a total addict, and I'm never going to sell a share." — Daily Journal annual meeting. On the $1.50 hot dog: "Anybody else would have raised the price of hotdogs a long time ago. They just don't do it… they know they've got something going there that's worth extra money to them, and they just don't destroy it." — 2023 *Acquired* interview.
- **What happened later.** He also said: "I've always believed that nothing was worth an infinite price. So even an admirable place like Costco could get to a price where you would say that's too high." And he acknowledged that buying at the prevailing price was becoming harder.
- **Useful analogies.** Holding a good business indefinitely, handling a partner’s disagreement with your favorite idea, and whether to raise a price a company can clearly charge more for.
- **Sources.** https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/04/munger-reflects-on-the-investment-warren-buffett-passed-on-costco.html https://finance.yahoo.com/news/berkshire-hathaways-charlie-munger-had-a-longtime-special-relationship-with-costco-223417032.html · Confidence: firsthand words transcribed by secondary sources.

## C11 · 2004 · Entrusting $88 million to Li Lu

- **Situation.** He had never entrusted money to an outside fund manager. After a long Thanksgiving lunch with Li Lu in 2003, Li was about to launch a new fund.
- **What he did.** In 2004 he invested $88 million of family money alongside Li’s $4 million and $50 million from others. The condition was that the fund close to new investors. He advised Li to convert the hedge fund into a long-only value fund. He considered inviting Li to Berkshire, but Li did not go.
- **His own explanation.** "In my life, I've given money to one outside manager, and that's Li Lu." "I'm not interested in revolution. I'm a capitalist. It was his capitalist aptitude that attracted me, not his revolutionary history." — *FT*, 2023.
- **What happened later.** He said the investment quadrupled or quintupled ("unholy good returns for a long, long time"). Another calculation put that gain over 19 years at just over 8% annualized, roughly in line with US equity indices. Li introduced him to BYD (C12) and is thought to have influenced his Alibaba purchase (C15).
- **Useful analogies.** Whether to entrust someone with money, how to assess them, and whether one trusted information channel can produce both your best and your worst ideas.
- **Sources.** https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/stocks/charlie-munger-li-lu-investment-stocks-himalaya-byd-buffett-berkshire-2023-9 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalaya_Capital · Confidence: corroborated by secondary sources.

## C12 · 2008 · BYD: his proudest investment at Berkshire

- **Situation.** A Chinese manufacturer of batteries and phone components wanted to make electric cars. Buffett did not invest in things he did not understand, and openly said he understood neither batteries nor phones nor cars.
- **What he did.** After Li Lu introduced him to Wang Chuanfu, he strongly urged Buffett to invest. Buffett sent David Sokol to China to inspect the factory. Berkshire wanted 25%, but Wang would sell only 10%. The final price was $230 million.
- **His own explanation.** "This guy is a combination of Thomas Edison and Jack Welch – something like Edison in solving technical problems, and something like Welch in getting done what he needs to do. I have never seen anything like it." — *Fortune*, 2009. "I have never helped do anything in Berkshire that was as good as BYD and I only did it once." — Daily Journal annual meeting, 2023.
- **What happened later.** The holding peaked at about $9.5 billion on paper. He did not present it as foresight. About Wang’s insistence on making cars he said "We tried to talk [Wang] out of doing what worked so well, which shows that there's some accident in life.". A month before his death he said "I may be a big fan, but I'm hanging on by my hat… They make me nervous. So aggressive.". His Daily Journal sold part at the end of 2021. Berkshire began reducing its holding in 2022 and sold out in 2025, after he died. Sokol, who had inspected the factory, left Berkshire in 2011 over the Lubrizol affair.
- **Useful analogies.** How to invest in an industry you do not understand by assessing the person, what happens when a founder ignores investors’ advice, and luck’s share in a big win.
- **Sources.** https://www.cnbc.com/2009/04/13/fortune-puts-warren-buffett-in-car-of-the-future-drivers-seat.html https://fortune.com/2023/02/16/charlie-munger-byd-stake-warren-buffett-berkshire-hathaway-china-tesla https://www.kingswell.io/p/charlie-munger-q-and-a-2023-daily · Confidence: firsthand words transcribed by secondary sources.

## C13 · 2009 · Putting 70% of cash into bank stocks in one quarter

- **Situation.** Daily Journal, which he chaired, had kept only cash and Treasuries for years. Bank investors were fleeing in the worst of the financial crisis in the first quarter of 2009.
- **What he did.** He put 71% of the company’s cash into bank stocks. At the same time he moved the endowment at Good Samaritan Hospital, where he chaired the board, from cash and short-term debt to corporate bonds after their sharp fall.
- **His own explanation.** At the annual meeting he said "we behaved pretty sensibly" and called prices "ridiculously low". Columnist Jason Zweig asked how he could buy while the world was falling apart. He replied "What would be hard about that?".
- **What happened later.** The unrealized gain reached 60% by March 31. Including later purchases, $49.7 million invested was worth $128.4 million by 2014. In contrast, he did nothing in the April 2020 pandemic crash, comparing himself to a captain in a typhoon and saying "we'd rather come out of it with a whole lot of liquidity".
- **Useful analogies.** When to use cash, whether to buy while others panic, and when to act or stand still.
- **Sources.** https://jasonzweig.com/charlie-munger-lessons-from-an-investing-giant https://www.wsj.com/articles/charlie-munger-the-phone-is-not-ringing-off-the-hook-11587132006 · Confidence: secondary WSJ column and primary interview.

## C14 · 2010 · “Suck it in and cope”

- **Situation.** After the financial crisis, the public was angry about bank rescues, while some wanted help for individual homeowners who could not pay. He spoke with students at the University of Michigan.
- **What he did.** He publicly defended rescuing banks and opposed blanket rescues for individuals. He drew an analogy with Weimar Germany’s failure to stabilize its financial system and Hitler’s subsequent rise.
- **His own explanation.** "There's danger in just shoveling out money to people who say, 'My life is a little harder than it used to be.' At a certain place you've got to say to the people, 'Suck it in and cope, buddy. Suck it in and cope.'" "if you just start bailing out all the individuals instead of telling them to adapt, the culture dies." — University of Michigan, 2010-09-14.
- **What happened later.** The comments provoked an uproar. Critics noted he and Berkshire had benefited from the bailouts and found the message unpersuasive coming from a billionaire. No apology or retraction was located.
- **Useful analogies.** An argument can have merit but lose credibility because of who makes it. The episode reveals an empathy gap in policy debate. His bluntness on such matters can hurt people. Include that when describing his stance.
- **Sources.** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2010-09-20/berkshire-s-munger-says-cash-strapped-should-suck-it-in-not-get-bailout https://www.businessinsider.com/charlie-mungers-best-quotes-2014-9 · Confidence: firsthand words transcribed by secondary sources.

## C15 · 2021 · Alibaba: buying with borrowed money and halving the stake the next year

- **Situation.** Alibaba’s share price halved after Jack Ma criticized regulators at the end of 2020. Daily Journal had not added a new US-listed stock since 2013.
- **What he did.** It started buying in the first quarter of 2021 and added in the third and fourth quarters to 600,000 shares, worth about $72 million and almost 30% of the portfolio. Fourth-quarter purchases used a margin loan. It sold half in the first quarter of 2022.
- **His own explanation.** "I regard Alibaba as one of the worst mistakes I ever made. In thinking about Alibaba, I got charmed with the idea of their position in the Chinese internet and I didn't stop to realize that it's still a goddamn retailer. It's going to be a competitive business, the internet. It's not going to be a cakewalk for everybody." — Daily Journal annual meeting, 2023. Two weeks before his death, he phrased it differently on CNBC. It was a decent company that had been overhyped, and Jack Ma mishandled government relations.
- **What happened later.** At the same meeting he said "I keep rubbing my own nose in my own mistakes, like I'm doing now, because I think it's good for myself.". Critics went further: a lifelong opponent of borrowing to trade stocks used leverage here, and Costco, which he loved for thirty years, was also a retailer. Alibaba shares recovered appreciably after his death.
- **Useful analogies.** Being dazzled by a story about a company’s status and overlooking the business itself, buying more as the price falls, and what it takes to acknowledge an error properly.
- **Sources.** https://www.kingswell.io/p/charlie-munger-q-and-a-2023-daily https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/stocks/charlie-munger-daily-journal-byd-alibaba-stock-portfolio-margin-debt-2022-2 Final CNBC interview, 2023-11-14. · Confidence: primary evidence.

## C16 · 2021–2023 · Munger Hall: disregarding architects

- **Situation.** The University of California, Santa Barbara needed student housing. Architecture had been his hobby for decades. He had helped fund and design a University of Michigan graduate residence with windowless bedrooms, which had been built and which he considered successful.
- **What he did.** He pledged $200 million conditional on building to his plans: eleven floors, roughly 4,500 residents, and 94% of single bedrooms with artificial windows in place of real ones. He reasoned that small rooms would draw students into common areas to socialize. In October 2021 Dennis McFadden, consulting architect to the university’s design review committee, resigned in protest, saying he could not support it as an architect, parent, or person.
- **His own explanation.** "When an ignorant man leaves, I regard it as a plus, not a minus." He called the architect an "idiot". "It's quite endurable, especially with good ventilation. Nobody minds going into a basement restroom and peeing because there's no window." "Architecture is a game of trade-offs."
- **What happened later.** Architectural criticism around the US and student petitions persisted two years. The university abandoned the plans in August 2023 and Munger died three months later. No reversal of his position was located.
- **Useful analogies.** Forceful multidisciplinary reasoning in your own hobby when practitioners collectively oppose it, and donations tied to unalterable plans. This is a failure case for his method and should be described accurately.
- **Sources.** https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2021/11/22/nightmare-of-the-windowless-dorm-room https://www.independent.com/2021/11/05/ucsb-munger-respond-to-avalanche-of-backlash-over-dormzilla https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munger_Hall · Confidence: corroborated by secondary sources.

## C17 · 2013–2023 · Cryptocurrency: ten years of escalating language

- **Situation.** Bitcoin went from a niche pursuit to a trillion-dollar asset class. Institutions and friends around him entered one after another.
- **What he did.** Beginning in 2013, when he called it "rat poison", he criticized it at annual meetings and used harsher language each year. He never owned it or presented a public technical analysis. In February 2023, he wrote in *The Wall Street Journal* urging the US to ban crypto by law and saying China deserved thanks for its precedent.
- **His own explanation.** "It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, I can't be left out." — Berkshire annual meeting, 2018. "disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization" — Berkshire annual meeting, 2021.
- **What happened later.** By his death Bitcoin’s price stood far above where it was when he started condemning it. Critics including Wolfgang Munchau argued his opposition rested on politics and morality rather than investment analysis, and that no other investor of comparable standing would dismiss a whole asset class outright. He responded by intensifying his language.
- **Useful analogies.** Cryptocurrency, meme assets, and assets supported solely by rising prices. His stance is clear, but this is where he was most emotional and offered the least argument. State that he conducted no technical analysis and that the price did not move as his remarks implied.
- **Sources.** *Why America Should Ban Crypto*, WSJ, 2023-02. https://fortune.com/2021/12/03/charlie-munger-criticizes-crypto-china/ https://www.dlnews.com/articles/people-culture/charlie-munger-was-shrill-on-crypto-but-sage-on-investing/ · Confidence: primary article and secondary transcriptions.
