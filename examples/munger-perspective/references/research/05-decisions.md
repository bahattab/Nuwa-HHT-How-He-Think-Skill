## 1955 · The death of his son after his divorce

- [Action] He divorced in 1953 at 29. His former wife kept the house and most of the property. A year later his eight-year-old son Teddy was diagnosed with leukemia. With no health insurance, Munger paid all the bills himself while continuing to practice law and care for his other children. Teddy died at nine, when Munger was 31.
- [Third-party account] His friend Rick Guerin recalled that after holding his son at the hospital, Munger walked alone through Pasadena crying.
- [Action] In 1956 he married Nancy Borthwick, whom he met on a blind date.
- Sources: Janet Lowe's Damn Right! (2000), cited by <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/charlie-munger-lost-9-old-155612903.html> and <https://africa.businessinsider.com/news/charlie-munger-first-met-warren-buffett-in-1959-heres-how-the-lawyer-became-an/9xypwl1> · Cross-checked secondary sources.
- [Statement] A retrospective account recorded by Lowe, not his words at the time: "Whenever you think that some situation or some person is ruining your life, it's actually you who are ruining your life… Feeling like a victim is a perfectly disastrous way to go through life." Source: <https://www.danielscrivner.com/damn-right-behind-the-scenes-of-berkshire-hathaway-and-charlie-munger-by-janet-lowe>

## 1962 · Founding a law firm and designing its compensation system

- [Action] He left Musick, Peeler & Garrett and co-founded Munger, Tolles, Hills & Rickershauser, now Munger, Tolles & Olson. With Rod Hills and Roy Tolles he devised the allocation method. Each January, each partner receives a form listing everyone and allocates an amount to each person, including themselves. The total must equal the year's net profit. There are no points, seniority bonuses, or compensation committee. After voting, everyone can see everyone else's allocations.
- [Third-party account] Partner Bart Williams: "It's the most humbling thing I've ever done here."
- Sources: Lowe's Damn Right!, summarized at <https://commoncog.com/c/cases/mto-incentives> · Cross-checked secondary sources.

## 1962–1975 · The Wheeler, Munger partnership fund

- [Action] He started the fund with Jack Wheeler in 1962. It ran highly concentrated positions and sometimes used leverage. When the Canadian government agreed to buy British Columbia Power at about $22 while it traded near $19, he put the partnership's capital, all his own money, and everything he could borrow into that single arbitrage (Schroeder, The Snowball).
- Results: No losing year in the first 11 years. The fund fell 31.9% in 1973 and 31.5% in 1974. $1,000 at the start of 1973 was worth $467 at the start of 1975, versus $688 for the Dow. Partners also withdrew 0.5% a month, and the book value shrank by more than 53%. The 1975 return was +73.2%. Annualized return for 1962–75 was 19.8%, or 13.7% net to limited partners, against 5.0% for the Dow.
- [Action] He began winding down the partnership after it recovered in 1975 and finished in 1976. He no longer managed money for outside partners afterward.
- Sources: Buffett's table in The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville (1984), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Lowe's appendix, and summaries at <https://mungerarchive.com/track-record> and <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/lessons-charlie-mungers-early-partnership-164823987.html> · Primary numbers and secondary accounts.
- [Inference] The earlier claim that it collapsed because of the same strategy is inaccurate. Quoted values fell by half before a large rebound the following year, and the overall record still far exceeded the market. What he ended was the business of managing other people's money.

## 1969 · People v. Belous

- [Action] He read that Dr. Leon Belous had been convicted after referring a pregnant patient to an abortion provider and persuaded Buffett to share the legal fees. With partners Rod Hills and Jim Adler he organized two amicus briefs. He wrote one himself, with 17 prominent attorneys signing on. Another was signed by 178 medical-school deans and professors. In September 1969 the California Supreme Court ruled 4–3 that the law was unconstitutional, the first such ruling by a major U.S. court on an anti-abortion law. The Roe v. Wade appeal brief later cited it. Munger served for years as trustee and treasurer of Planned Parenthood in Los Angeles.
- [Statement] "It was emotionally hard for me to become pro-choice because I do have reverence for human life, but when I thought through the consequences, I found it necessary to overrule that part of my nature."
- [Third-party account] Buffett: "Charlie took over the case… Charlie did all the work on it night and day, even writing some of the briefs himself."
- Sources: Lowe's Damn Right!, <http://www.metnews.com/articles/2023/munger_112923.htm>, and the judgment at <https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/people-v-belous-22692> · Cross-checked secondary accounts and primary judgment.

## 1972 · See's Candies

- [Action] Blue Chip Stamps adviser Robert Flaherty told Munger about See's, and Munger told Buffett. The seller wanted $30 million, with about $10 million in cash on the books. Annual pretax profit was about $4 million and net tangible assets about $8 million. The two offered a ceiling of $25 million, which the seller accepted. Prices rose after Christmas nearly every year thereafter, from about $1.85–$1.96 a pound in 1972 to $8.80 in 1997.
- [Statement] To Fortune: "Ira really shamed us. Warren and I were too cheap." His partner Ira Marshall had rebuked them at the time, saying that some things were worth paying more for.
- [Statement] At Berkshire's 2017 annual meeting: "it would have been very wise to buy See's Candy at a slightly higher price. You know if they'd asked it, we wouldn't have done it, so we've gotten a lot of credit for being smarter than we were." Buffett added that he would not have paid $5 million more, but Charlie would have.
- [Statement] At the same meeting: "We were very lucky that, early, the habit of buying horrible businesses because they were really cheap. It gave us a lot of experience trying to fix unfixable businesses as they headed downward toward doom… So, I would argue that our early stupidity helped us."
- Sources: <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/why-buffett-and-munger-berkshire-hathaway-love-companies-like-sees-candies-low-capital-145432726.html>, <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/learn-buffetts-sees-candies-deal-173114333.html>, and <https://thescienceofhitting.com/p/there-arent-many-sees> · Primary remarks transcribed in secondary sources.

## 1977 · Belridge Oil

- [Action] A broker phoned with 300 Belridge Oil shares at $115 apiece. Munger thought it was far below liquidation value and bought them. Three days later the broker offered another 1,500 at the same price. This time Munger would have had to sell something else to raise about $170,000, so he declined. In 1979 Shell bought Belridge for about $3,700 a share.
- [Statement] At Berkshire's 2001 annual meeting: "The guy called me back three days later and offered me 1,500 more shares. But this time I had to sell something to buy the damn Belridge Oil. That mistake, if you trace it through, has cost me $200 million. And it was all because I had to go to a slight inconvenience and sell something."
- [Statement] At the same meeting: "The mistakes that have been most extreme in Berkshire's history are mistakes of omission. They don't show up in our figures. They show up in opportunity costs."
- [Statement] On the 2021 Think with Pinker podcast: "I just know that if I rub my nose in my own mistakes, I'm less likely to commit new ones of the same type… It works in training a dog and so I think it would work on me."
- Sources: <https://www.investing.com/analysis/autozone-the-mistakes-of-omission-200590798> and <https://www.businessinsider.com/charlie-munger-billions-berkshire-hathaway-investing-mistakes-alibaba-belridge-oil-2023-11> · Primary remarks transcribed in secondary sources.
- Conflict: Some accounts say the broker called the next day and Munger declined because he disliked the CEO. Munger himself said in 2001 that it was three days later and that he found selling another asset inconvenient. This account follows his own version.

## Good Samaritan Hospital (year unconfirmed, as recounted in Lowe's 2000 book)

- [Action] Soon after joining the board, he concluded that the hospital was mismanaged. The board routinely rubber-stamped medical staff decisions, which often protected some doctors' financial interests. The issue was in cardiovascular care. With a physician friend's help, Munger first studied mortality and complication rates under different practices. He then moved to reverse a ruling by the organized medical staff on patient safety grounds. It passed 17–2 and the chair resigned. As the instigator of the reform, Munger took over as chair.
- [Statement] "The existing chairman did not agree with me. I proposed a resolution reversing a ruling of the organized medical staff on the ground that the staff was endangering the health and safety of our patients. With some doctors on the board voting yes, it passed 17 to 2." About the previous chair: "He was an able man, and understandably was reluctant, as a layman, to reverse what purported to be medical decisions."
- [Third-party account] Andrew Leeka, who later applied to run the hospital, heard that it was “like Bosnia,” partly because Munger called executive committee meetings every few weeks, keeping management under constant pressure.
- [Action] During the 2009 financial crisis he shifted most of the hospital endowment out of cash and short-term bonds, where it had long sat, into corporate bonds after they plunged (Zweig, WSJ, 2014).
- Sources: Lowe's Damn Right!, chapter 19, and <https://jasonzweig.com/charlie-munger-lessons-from-an-investing-giant> · Single secondary account, including Munger's own remarks.

## 1989 · Leaving the savings and loan trade group

- [Action] During the savings and loan crisis, Mutual Savings, a Wesco subsidiary Munger chaired, sent a letter on May 30, 1989, withdrawing from the U.S. League of Savings Institutions, an industry lobbying group. The letter received broad coverage and appeared in Berkshire's annual report that year. Mutual Savings supported almost all of the FIRREA reforms even though many would hurt its own profits.
- [Statement] In the letter: "The league responds to the savings and loan mess as Exxon would have responded to the oil spill from the Valdez if it had insisted thereafter on liberal use of whiskey by tanker captains." "It is not unfair to liken the situation now facing Congress to cancer and to liken the league to a significant carcinogenic agent."
- [Action] Around the same time, in 1988, he used Mutual Savings' funds to buy 2.4 million Freddie Mac shares, then subject to a 4% cap per holder. The average cost was $29.89, versus $67.12 at the end of 1989.
- Sources: <https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1989-06-11-fi-3019-story.html>, Berkshire's 1989 shareholder letter at <https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1989.html>, and Wesco's 1989 letter at <https://www.gurufocus.com/news/2137397/charlie-mungers-1989-letter-to-wesco-shareholders> · Primary.

## 1991 · Salomon

- [Action] In 1987 he joined Salomon's board alongside Buffett. In August 1991, it emerged that management had known for months that trader Mozer had improperly bid for Treasury securities but had not reported it. On the phone that Saturday Munger called the delay "thumb sucking".
- [Third-party account] Buffett's 2024 shareholder letter (February 2025): "The cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes or what Charlie Munger called 'thumb-sucking.' Problems, he would tell me, cannot be wished away. They require action, however uncomfortable that may be."
- Sources: Fortune's 1997 Warren Buffett's Wild Ride at Salomon and <https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/27/warren-buffett-charlie-munger-taught-me-to-quickly-admit-mistakes.html> · Cross-checked secondary sources.
- Related primary material: In his talk on misjudgment, Munger described See's employees caught embezzling: "they always say, 'I never did it before, and I'm never gonna do it again.' And we cashier them. It would be evil not to because terrible behavior spreads." <https://jamesclear.com/great-speeches/psychology-of-human-misjudgment-by-charlie-munger>

## 1997– · Costco

- [Action] He learned the business through Price Club founder Sol Price and had invested before Price Club and Costco merged in 1993. Costco invited Buffett to be an independent director, but Buffett declined and referred Munger. Munger served from 1997 until his death. He urged Buffett to buy a large position alongside him, but Buffett did not follow. Berkshire held about 1% and sold all 4.3 million shares in 2020. Munger personally sold none and held about 187,000 shares in 2023.
- [Statement] At a Daily Journal annual meeting: "I love everything about Costco. I'm a total addict, and I'm never going to sell a share." Two reports date it to the 2022 and 2023 meetings, respectively. He also said around that time: "I've always believed that nothing was worth an infinite price. So even an admirable place like Costco could get to a price where you would say that's too high."
- [Statement] On Acquired in October 2023: "Take those goddamned Costco hotdogs… Anybody else would have raised the price of hotdogs a long time ago. They just don't do it… they know they've got something going there that's worth extra money to them, and they just don't destroy it."
- Sources: <https://www.cnbc.com/2023/11/04/munger-reflects-on-the-investment-warren-buffett-passed-on-costco.html> and <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/berkshire-hathaways-charlie-munger-had-a-longtime-special-relationship-with-costco-223417032.html> · Primary remarks transcribed in secondary sources.
- Correction to the earlier version: He did not first buy Costco in 1997. That was the year he joined the board.

## 2004 · Entrusting family capital to Li Lu

- [Action] They met around 1996 and had a long Thanksgiving lunch conversation in 2003. Li Lu started a new fund in 2004 with $4 million of his own, $50 million from others, and $88 million from Munger's family, on condition that the fund close to new investors. At Munger's suggestion, it shifted from a hedge fund to long-only value investing. He once tried to recruit Li Lu to Berkshire, but Li declined.
- [Statement] In the FT in 2023: "We made unholy good returns for a long, long time. That $88 million has become four or five times that." "I'm not interested in revolution. I'm a capitalist. It was his capitalist aptitude that attracted me, not his revolutionary history." Also: "In my life, I've given money to one outside manager, and that's Li Lu."
- Sources: The FT's 2023 profile of Li Lu, cited at <https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/stocks/charlie-munger-li-lu-investment-stocks-himalaya-byd-buffett-berkshire-2023-9> and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalaya_Capital> · Cross-checked secondary sources.
- Third-party account: A four- to fivefold return over 19 years equates to about 8.5% annualized. A commenter claimed this was similar to the S&P over the same period. The comment thread is not a professional source and is noted only to record that the challenge exists.

## 2008 · BYD

- [Action] Li Lu invested in BYD in 2002 and introduced Munger to Wang Chuanfu. Munger strongly urged Buffett to invest in 2008. Buffett said he did not understand mobile phones, batteries, or cars, and sent David Sokol to inspect the factory in China. Berkshire wanted 25%, but Wang would sell only 10%, for $230 million or HK$8 per share.
- [Statement] To Fortune in 2009: "This guy is a combination of Thomas Edison and Jack Welch – something like Edison in solving technical problems, and something like Welch in getting done what he needs to do. I have never seen anything like it."
- [Statement] At the February 2023 Daily Journal annual meeting: "I have never helped do anything in Berkshire that was as good as BYD and I only did it once." At the same meeting, on Wang Chuanfu's insistence on making cars: "We tried to talk [Wang] out of doing what worked so well, which shows that there's some accident in life."
- [Statement] On Acquired in October 2023: "I may be a big fan, but I'm hanging on by my hat while he lurches around the track. They make me nervous. So aggressive."
- Later: Daily Journal sold about $50 million of BYD shares at the end of 2021. Berkshire started reducing its stake in August 2022 and fully exited in 2025, after Munger's death. Sokol left Berkshire in 2011 after the Lubrizol incident.
- Sources: <https://www.cnbc.com/2009/04/13/fortune-puts-warren-buffett-in-car-of-the-future-drivers-seat.html>, <https://fortune.com/2023/02/16/charlie-munger-byd-stake-warren-buffett-berkshire-hathaway-china-tesla>, and <https://www.kingswell.io/p/charlie-munger-q-and-a-2023-daily> · Primary remarks transcribed in secondary sources.

## 2009 · Daily Journal buys bank stocks at the bottom

- [Action] In the first quarter of 2009, he put 71% of Daily Journal's cash into bank stocks. ValueWalk inferred these were mainly Wells Fargo and U.S. Bancorp, with Bank of America later. The unrealized gain was already 60% by March 31. Purchases, including later ones, totaled $49.7 million and were worth $128.4 million in August 2014. The books had previously held only cash and Treasuries for years.
- [Statement] At the annual meeting, he said "we behaved pretty sensibly" and bought heavily when prices were "ridiculously low". When Zweig asked how he could act with the world seemingly collapsing, he replied: "What would be hard about that?"
- Sources: <https://jasonzweig.com/charlie-munger-lessons-from-an-investing-giant> and <https://www.cnbc.com/2017/11/03/author-zweig-on-what-makes-buffett-the-greatest-investor-of-all-time-how-to-trade-bitcoin.html> · Secondary reporting (WSJ column).

## 2010 · “Suck it in and cope”

- [Statement] At a University of Michigan discussion on September 14, 2010, he defended the 2008 financial-sector bailouts: "You should thank God" for bank bailouts. "if you just start bailing out all the individuals instead of telling them to adapt, the culture dies" "There's danger in just shoveling out money to people who say, 'My life is a little harder than it used to be.' At a certain place you've got to say to the people, 'Suck it in and cope, buddy. Suck it in and cope.'" He drew a parallel to Weimar Germany's failure to stabilize its financial system and Hitler's eventual rise.
- [Third-party account] Public reaction was fierce. Commentators noted that he himself had benefited from the bailouts.
- Sources: <https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2010-09-20/berkshire-s-munger-says-cash-strapped-should-suck-it-in-not-get-bailout> and <https://www.businessinsider.com/charlie-mungers-best-quotes-2014-9> · Primary remarks transcribed in secondary sources.
- Correction: He said it in September 2010, not during the 2008 financial crisis.

## 2020 · Standing still during the pandemic

- [Statement] In April 2020, to the WSJ: "We're like the captain of a ship when the worst typhoon that's ever happened comes. We just want to get through the typhoon, and we'd rather come out of it with a whole lot of liquidity." "We're not playing, 'Oh goody, goody, everything's going to hell, let's plunge 100% of the reserves [into buying businesses].'" The headline quoted him: "The phone is not ringing off the hook".
- Source: <https://www.wsj.com/articles/charlie-munger-the-phone-is-not-ringing-off-the-hook-11587132006> · Primary interview.
- [Inference] Contrast the aggressive buying in 2009. The same person went all in once and stood still the other time. In 2009 prices were already absurdly low, whereas in April 2020 nobody was calling for help.

## 2021–2023 · Alibaba

- [Action] Daily Journal began buying Alibaba ADS in the first quarter of 2021, spending about $40 million. Li Lu's view was reportedly one reason. It added 83% in the third quarter and nearly doubled the stake again in the fourth, to 600,000 shares worth about $72 million and nearly 30% of its U.S. equity portfolio. The fourth-quarter purchase used margin loans. It sold half, 302,060 shares, in the first quarter of 2022. Munger stepped down as Daily Journal chair in March 2022.
- [Statement] At the February 2023 annual meeting: "I regard Alibaba as one of the worst mistakes I ever made. In thinking about Alibaba, I got charmed with the idea of their position in the Chinese internet and I didn't stop to realize that it's still a goddamn retailer. It's going to be a competitive business, the internet. It's not going to be a cakewalk for everybody." Asked at the same meeting which good idea he had killed that year, he said: "it wasn't a good idea, it was a bad idea… I got over-charmed by the people who were leading the online retailing and I didn't realize it's still retailing… I keep rubbing my own nose in my own mistakes, like I'm doing now, because I think it's good for myself."
- [Statement] In his final CNBC interview on November 14, 2023, he identified the Alibaba bought for the Munger family as his worst investment: "which is a pretty good company. But I think it got over-hyped. And Jack Ma… made mistakes in dealing with the Chinese government."
- Later: Alibaba shares rebounded materially after he died. Yahoo Finance reported in 2025 that they had risen 74% since his death.
- Sources: <https://www.kingswell.io/p/charlie-munger-q-and-a-2023-daily>, <https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/stocks/charlie-munger-daily-journal-byd-alibaba-stock-portfolio-margin-debt-2022-2>, <https://blog.umd.edu/davidkass/2023/12/01/5-highlights-of-charlie-munger-interview-by-becky-quick-on-cnbc>, and <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/charlie-munger-called-alibaba-one-230116375.html> · Primary.
- Conflict: His explanations moved from forgetting it was still a retailer to its being overhyped and Jack Ma mishandling government relations. The earlier notes' claim that policy risk was the real cause was an outside inference, not Munger's own account.

## 2021–2023 · UCSB Munger Hall

- [Action] Pledged $200 million to the University of California, Santa Barbara, for a project estimated at $1.5 billion, on condition it follow his plans: 11 floors, 1.68 million square feet, around 4,500 students, and artificial rather than real windows in 94% of single bedrooms. His rationale was that smaller, windowless bedrooms would encourage students to spend time together in common areas. In October 2021, design review committee consulting architect Dennis McFadden resigned in protest, calling it "unsupportable from my perspective as an architect, a parent and a human being".
- [Statement] "When an ignorant man leaves, I regard it as a plus, not a minus." In Architectural Record he called McFadden an "idiot". "It's quite endurable, especially with good ventilation. Nobody minds going into a basement restroom and peeing because there's no window." To MarketWatch: "Architecture is a game of trade-offs."
- Later: The university abandoned the proposal in August 2023. A University of Michigan graduate residence he funded earlier, with a 2013 gift of $110 million and similarly windowless bedrooms, was built and occupied. He had said, "You never saw a happier bunch of students".
- Sources: <https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2021/11/22/nightmare-of-the-windowless-dorm-room>, <https://www.independent.com/2021/11/05/ucsb-munger-respond-to-avalanche-of-backlash-over-dormzilla>, and <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munger_Hall> · Cross-checked secondary sources.

## Differences from the earlier account

- Overturned: See's was not a case of Munger wisely persuading Buffett to pay up for quality. Both thought it expensive, and a slightly higher asking price would have killed the deal. Partner Ira Marshall set them straight. Munger said they got more credit than their judgment deserved.
- Overturned: The partnership did not collapse because of its concentrated strategy. It gained 73.2% the next year and returned 19.8% annualized overall, versus 5% for the Dow. It was managing other people's money that he ended.
- Refined: The earlier work remembered each of the 1973–74 losses as about 30 points and the combined decline as about half. The annual figures, withdrawal terms, and his use of leverage for arbitrage are now documented.
- Refined: Alibaba purchases used margin. There are two formulations of his admission, and his final interview offered another explanation.
- Refined: On BYD, Berkshire was refused a 25% stake, sent Sokol to inspect the plant, advised Wang Chuanfu against making cars, and in later life Munger said it made him nervous.
- Added: Belridge Oil. He considered his failure to buy more because of the inconvenience his stupidest mistake.
- Added: The law firm's peer-allocation form.
- Added: Belous. He personally wrote a brief supporting abortion rights and said he had overruled part of his own nature.
- Added: Good Samaritan Hospital. He checked mortality rates before overturning a medical staff decision.
- Added: The 1989 public letter leaving the savings and loan trade group.
- Added: In 2009 he put 71% of Daily Journal's cash into bank shares in one move, but stood entirely still in 2020.
- Added: The condition for giving Li Lu $88 million was that the fund stop taking new money.
- Added: Munger had invested before the 1993 Costco merger, Buffett never joined him, and Berkshire sold its entire stake in 2020.
