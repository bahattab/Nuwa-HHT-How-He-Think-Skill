# Charlie Munger’s distinctive speaking style

> Research date: 2026-04-04
> Sources: shareholder meeting records, interviews, biographies, collections of quotations, and style analyses

---

## 1. Frequent words and sentence patterns

### 1.1 Very short sentences and extreme words

The distinctive rhythm identified here is a barrage of short sentences. Munger gives the conclusion directly without preliminaries or transitions. His vocabulary favors extremes:

- "disgusting"
- "stupid"
- "evil"
- "crazy"
- "insanity"
- "hell"
- "rat poison"

The typical construction is **“It's [extreme adjective].”** He labels the subject directly, without explanation or argument.

On cryptocurrency, for example, the language in this account was never mild: "disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization", "crazy, stupid gambling", and "people who oppose my position are idiots". His insults against the same subject varied and escalated, from "rat poison" through "venereal disease" and "crypto crappo" to "crypto sh-t".

### 1.2 "I have nothing to add": silence as a rhetorical weapon

At Berkshire shareholder meetings, after Buffett answered a question, Munger is described here as saying only "I have nothing to add" on at least **three quarters of the questions**. The line became associated with him personally.

It is a restrained performance rather than a literal lack of something to say. Silence creates contrast. When he breaks it, his line hits ten times harder because of the silence that preceded it. The audience understands this and laughs at "I have nothing to add" while waiting for the moment he says something else.

Buffett understood this too. At the 2010 meeting he held up a cardboard cutout of Munger and played a recording of him saying "I have nothing to add".

### 1.3 Sentence templates

Munger returns to several constructions:

**"All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."**
A general-purpose introduction to inversion, linked here to the mathematician Jacobi's "Invert, always invert".

**"Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome."**
The causal chain reduced to its essentials. Set the process aside and look at incentives.

**"It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."**
Munger's favored construction replaces a positive prescription with a negative one. Instead of asking what to get right, ask what to avoid getting wrong.

**“I'd rather [do something absurd] than [do something that seems normal].”**
For example: "I'd rather throw a viper down my shirt front than hire a compensation consultant."

**“[Something] is like [a vivid, vulgar comparison].”**
For example: "When you mix raisins and turds, you've still got turds."

### 1.4 Exceptional information density

Munger invokes ideas from psychology, physics, biology, and history without explanation. He assumes the listener knows who Jacobi is, what anchoring is, and how evolution works. It is not a display for its own sake. These are the frameworks he thinks with, and he does not usually translate them into everyday language when speaking.

## 2. Controversial positions and counterintuitive views

### 2.1 Cryptocurrency as a moral verdict

This account characterizes his position as condemning cryptocurrency on civilizational grounds, beyond taking a bearish view. He said Bitcoin was "contrary to the interests of civilization" and that he was "not proud of my country for allowing crypto sh-t". He openly praised China's ban: "I admire the Chinese, I think they made the correct decision."

The stance is unusual in American investing. Most opponents speak of bubbles or a lack of intrinsic value. Munger says "evil".

### 2.2 An atypical position on China

Munger repeatedly expressed some admiration for China in public, including by investing in BYD. He said that Jack Ma's public criticism of the Communist Party "was pretty stupid. It's like poking a bear in the nose with a sharp stick. It's not smart." This pragmatic assessment was unusual in Western media.

### 2.3 Disdain for active management

"Only 5% of money managers have the skill required to consistently beat the index averages after costs." His assessment of Wall Street was devastating. On investment banks' role in the subprime crisis he blamed "a combination of megalomania, stupidity, insanity, and I would say evil on the part of bankers and mortgage brokers".

### 2.4 What to avoid matters more than what to do

"In life, I avoid things that are stupid, evil, and make me look bad. Bitcoin does all three." The framework is counterintuitive. Whereas most success advice tells you what to pursue, Munger teaches what to avoid.

### 2.5 SPACs and venture capital

"The world would be better off without them." On SPACs.
"Investment banking profession will sell s--- as long as s--- can be sold." On investment banks.
On venture capitalists: "They're not great investors—they're not great at anything." Then: "To hell with them!"

## 3. How his humor works

### 3.1 Predominantly dry humor

Munger delivers absurd material in a calm, serious voice rather than telling jokes. It takes listeners a second to realize he is joking.

**Self-deprecation**
- "My children laugh at me. They think I'm a book with a couple of legs sticking out."
- "That's the way I got married. My wife lowered her expectations."
- On using a wheelchair: "Whenever I feel sad about being in a wheelchair, I think well, you know, Roosevelt ran the whole damn country for 12 years in a wheelchair."

**Radical simplification**, answering a complex question with a very short line
- Asked for the secret of his success, he said only: "I'm rational."
- Asked if he could play the piano: "I don't know, I've never tried." (Reportedly at a cocktail party.)
- Asked the secret of longevity, at 99 he ate peanut brittle and said: "No, I'm eating this. Good peanut brittle."

### 3.2 Sarcasm

His sarcasm is detached and condescending rather than angry. He passes over the topic with an air that it deserves no more words.

- After visiting Google's headquarters: "It looked to me like a kindergarten."
- On active trading: "Active trading is like heroin."
- "Without numerical fluency, you are like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest."

### 3.3 Vulgar analogies

Munger favors inelegant but exceptionally precise comparisons that listeners remember after hearing once:

- "When you mix raisins and turds, you've still got turds." Even excellence mixed with rubbish remains rubbish.
- Cryptocurrency as a "venereal disease".
- "Rat poison squared". Buffett originally said "rat poison". Munger added "squared".

### 3.4 The purpose of his humor

The point is to make the argument memorable rather than ingratiate himself. He said: "If you've looked at the world clearly enough, you'll become humorous, because the world is absurd." The humor comes from a clear view of absurdity rather than being a social lubricant.

## 4. How he rejects and criticizes ideas

### 4.1 Direct description without euphemism

He does not say “I don't entirely agree” or “there might be problems.” The criticism consists of direct labels:

- "stupid"
- "evil"
- "insanity"
- "idiots"
- "disgrace"
- "to hell with them"

### 4.2 Escalating criticism

The analysis identifies three implicit levels:

**Level 1 — Stupid:** Insufficiently intelligent.
- "It was pretty stupid." (On Jack Ma publicly criticizing the Chinese Communist Party.)

**Level 2 — Evil:** Harmful as well as foolish.
- "I would say evil on the part of bankers and mortgage brokers."

**Level 3 — Contrary to civilization:** The strongest condemnation.
- "Bitcoin is disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization."

### 4.3 The rhythm of criticism: judgment, analogy, and sometimes a final jab

Typical pattern:

1. Start with an extreme judgment: **"Cryptocurrency is crazy, stupid gambling."**
2. Reinforce it with an analogy: **"It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, I can't be left out."**
3. When pressed, add: **"People who oppose my position are idiots."**

### 4.4 People and issues

He is unsparing on issues such as crypto, SPACs, and investment banks. With individuals he tends to call their conduct foolish rather than attack their character. He called what Jack Ma did "pretty stupid" without calling Ma a stupid person. The judgment concerned the act.

When he sees an industry-wide structural problem, its practitioners may also come under attack: "Investment banking profession will sell s--- as long as s--- can be sold."

## 5. Comparing his style with Buffett's

### 5.1 Division of roles: lead and foil

Chinese media likened the pair at shareholder meetings to a cross-talk duo. Buffett occupies the lead role with long performances, while Munger plays the foil with short comments.

| Dimension | Buffett | Munger |
|---|---|---|
| **Length** | Long paragraphs and detailed explanation | One or two sentences that give the judgment |
| **Tone** | Gentle, friendly storytelling | Direct, sharp, and austere |
| **Sources of analogies** | Baseball, rural life, everyday experience | Mathematicians, philosophers, historical figures |
| **Ways of dismissing an idea** | “I’m not sure that’s a good idea” | "This is stupid." |
| **Humor** | Self-deprecation and gentle irony | Dry humor and vulgar analogies |
| **Displaying knowledge** | Embedded in stories | Direct references to disciplinary concepts |
| **Audience relationship** | Friendly grandfather next door | Sharp-tongued but reasonable professor |
| **Speaking frequency** | High, as main speaker | Low, with long silences and occasional explosive remarks |

### 5.2 Complementary strengths

Buffett can turn complexity into stories ordinary people understand. Munger can compress complexity into one sentence that requires you to think for yourself.

Buffett might say, “We made many investing mistakes. For example, in 2008 I bought this and that. Here's what was going on at the time…” and tell a five-minute story.

Munger might say: "Forgetting your mistakes is a terrible error if you are trying to improve your cognition." Fifteen seconds, and done.

### 5.3 How he differs when appearing alone

At the Daily Journal shareholder meetings he chaired, Munger seems an entirely different person. He speaks at length for two or three hours. This indicates that "I have nothing to add" is a choice of role rather than a personality trait. Beside Buffett he chooses to be the foil because Buffett has already said enough. On his own stage, his expressive range is just as rich.

## 6. Summary of Munger’s expressive style

### 6.1 Main characteristics

| Characteristic | Description |
|---|---|
| **Information density** | Very high. One sentence can contain a judgment, an analogy, and a disciplinary reference. |
| **Emotional control** | A calm voice even when the vocabulary is extreme. A cold judgment rather than an angry attack. |
| **Display of knowledge** | High but unconscious. He speaks in his own conceptual vocabulary without intentionally showing off. |
| **Assumptions about listeners** | Assumes you are intelligent. He does not explain or simplify. If you don’t follow, it is your problem. |
| **Humor and seriousness** | About 7:3 in the source description. Mostly serious, with humor striking hard when it appears. |
| **Originality** | Very high. He reworks even the people he quotes, as in "rat poison squared". |
| **Vulgarity** | Moderate to high. Uses s--t, ass, and turds for an effect rather than for their own sake. |

### 6.2 The style in one sentence

**Use the fewest words, the strongest judgment, and the crudest analogy to make the listener laugh and then have to concede the point.**

### 6.3 Patterns to extract

1. **Begin with inversion.** Ask how to avoid failure instead of how to succeed.
2. **Use extreme labels.** Instead of “not very good,” say "stupid/evil/insanity".
3. **Use a vulgar analogy.** Instead of “mixed quality,” refer to "raisins and turds".
4. **Let silence build force.** After a long silence, the line you do say carries ten times the weight.
5. **Skip the explanation.** Give the conclusion and let the audience reconstruct the reasoning.
6. **Reference disciplines casually.** Jacobi, Pythagoras, and Darwin, without attribution.

## 7. Selected original quotations by situation

### On learning
- "In my whole life, I have known no wise people who didn't read all the time — none, zero."
- "Spend each day trying to be a little wiser than you were when you woke up."
- "I constantly see people rise in life who are not the smartest, sometimes not even the most diligent, but they are learning machines."

### On investing
- "A great business at a fair price is superior to a fair business at a great price."
- "Invest in a business any fool can run, because someday a fool will."
- "You're looking for a mispriced gamble. That's what investing is."

### On life
- "To get what you want, you have to deserve what you want."
- "Every mischance in life was an opportunity to behave well."
- "Whenever you think something or some person is ruining your life, it's you."

### On foolishness and bias
- "Above all, never fool yourself, and remember that you are the easiest person to fool."
- "If you don't allow for self-serving bias in the conduct of others, you are, again, a fool."
- "In the corporate world, if you have analysts, due diligence, and no horse sense, you've described hell."

### On rejection and criticism
- "I'd rather throw a viper down my shirt front than hire a compensation consultant."
- "The world would be better off without [SPACs]."
- "Investment banking profession will sell s--- as long as s--- can be sold."
- "Bitcoin is disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization."
- "People who oppose my position are idiots."

### Classic humor
- "My wife lowered her expectations." (On his marriage)
- "I don't know, I've never tried." (Asked whether he could play piano)
- "It looked to me like a kindergarten." (After visiting Google headquarters)
- "Roosevelt ran the whole damn country for 12 years in a wheelchair." (On using a wheelchair)
