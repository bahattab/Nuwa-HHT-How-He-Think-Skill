# Charlie Munger: External criticism and blind spots

> Research date: 2026-04-04
> Purpose: Provide material on blind spots for Munger-related skills, rather than presenting only a favorable account.

---

## I. Patterns Munger may not have recognized or been willing to acknowledge

### 1. A systematic blind spot in technology

[Fortune / Bloomberg / Yahoo Finance] → Munger and Buffett missed early opportunities to invest in technology giants such as Google and Amazon. Munger admitted that he was embarrassed, saying, “I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google.” Ironically, GEICO was buying large numbers of Google ad clicks at the time. They had direct operating data showing how powerful Google's economics were, yet did not turn that operating insight into an investment thesis. → **Where this diverges from his self-description:** Munger championed multidisciplinary mental models and the circle of competence, but in technology, that circle became a justification for staying in his comfort zone. Despite his command of cognitive biases, he reproduced the anchoring effect himself. Anchored to traditional valuation frameworks, he could not assess network effects and platform economics.

### 2. Dismissing new things emotionally rather than analyzing them rationally

[DL News / Seeking Alpha] → Columnist Wolfgang Munchau argued that Munger's cryptocurrency opposition was fundamentally political rather than investment analysis. Munger's vocabulary included “rat poison,” “venereal disease,” “trading turds,” “disgusting,” and “immoral.” These are expressions of emotion, not rational arguments. Munchau's central criticism was, “No investor of his calibre would ever be so categorical about any asset class.” → **Where this diverges from his self-description:** Munger taught rational thinking and avoidance of ideological bias, yet his stance on cryptocurrency violated his own principles. A rational opponent would say that the risk–reward ratio is unattractive, not that the asset is rat poison. A younger Munger might at least have studied it seriously.

### 3. Dismissing AI in 2023

[Money Digest / City Journal] → At Berkshire's 2023 annual meeting, Munger said, “I think old-fashioned intelligence works pretty well.” He added that the idea of AI had existed since the 1950s, implying that it was nothing new. → **Where this diverges from his self-description:** Munger emphasized continuous learning and staying curious. Yet faced with what may be the greatest technological transformation of the 21st century, he exhibited selective disregard.

### 4. A desire for control disguised as generosity

[The Blum Firm / The Chronicle of Higher Education] → Munger attached strict conditions to nearly all his charitable gifts. The most prominent example was the Munger Hall dormitory proposal at UCSB. He donated $200 million but demanded construction according to his own plans: an eleven-story mega-dorm housing 4,500 students, with most rooms lacking windows. Dennis McFadden, an architect on UCSB's design review committee, resigned in protest, calling the design “unsupportable from my perspective as an architect, a parent, and a human being.” Architecture critic Paul Goldberger called it “a grotesque, sick joke — a jail masquerading as a dormitory.” Faced with widespread criticism, Munger called his critics “idiots.” → **Where this diverges from his self-description:** Munger saw himself as rational and receptive to feedback and evidence. In the Munger Hall case, however, he rejected input from architects, students, and the community, displaying authority bias, a cognitive bias on his own list of psychological misjudgments. The project was ultimately canceled in 2023.

## II. Major criticisms and controversies, ranked by severity

### Controversy 1: The Alibaba investment debacle

[Yibao / Fortune / Seeking Alpha] → Munger bought a large Alibaba stake through Daily Journal, later calling the investment “one of the worst mistakes I ever made.”

**Specific losses:**
- Five-year returns: BABA −59%, versus Amazon +109%, Costco +213%, and Walmart +86% over the same period.
- Alibaba's market capitalization fell to $184 billion, one-tenth of Amazon's.
- In Q1 2022, Daily Journal cut its position in half at a 20% loss.

**Why it went wrong:** Munger later acknowledged, “I got charmed by their position in the Chinese internet and didn't stop to realize, they're still a goddamn retailer.” Alibaba's internet aura dazzled him, and he overlooked the fact that it was a retailer. A deeper problem was his underestimation of political risk in China. Jack Ma faced a crackdown after criticizing financial regulators in 2020. A business environment where the party has the final say differs completely from the Western rule-of-law framework Munger knew.

**The irony:** Costco, a Munger favorite, returned 213% over the same period, and it is also a retailer. The problem wasn't that retail was bad. It was his misjudgment of China's political and economic environment.

### Controversy 2: His attitude toward China and the CCP

[Fortune / CNBC / CoinDesk] → Munger repeatedly praised Chinese policy in public. Writing about cryptocurrency in The Wall Street Journal, he said, “What should the U.S. do after a ban of cryptocurrencies is in place? Well, one more action might make sense: Thank the Chinese communist leader for his splendid example of uncommon sense.” Speaking of the cryptocurrency ban, he also said, “I admire the Chinese, I think they made the correct decision.”

**The central criticism:** A billionaire who built his whole fortune in the United States and benefited from American rule-of-law protections publicly urged the United States to follow the Chinese Communist Party's regulatory approach and commended an authoritarian government's “uncommon sense.” The stance triggered widespread discomfort and criticism.

**Relationship with Li Lu:** Munger's optimism about China was influenced substantially by Li Lu, the founder of Himalaya Capital. Li participated in the 1989 Tiananmen events before becoming a U.S. investor. He was Munger's adviser on investing in China and recommended the BYD investment. BYD was indeed a major success, generating more than $7 billion on a $230 million investment. But that one success may have reinforced Munger's excessive optimism about China.

### Controversy 3: His categorical rejection of cryptocurrency

[Fortune / Yahoo Finance / CNBC] → A selection of Munger's remarks about cryptocurrency:
- “noxious poison” and “rat poison”
- “stupid, immoral, and disgusting”
- “like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, I can't be left out”
- “I wish they'd never been invented”
- “I'm not proud of my country for allowing crypto sh-t”
- A Wall Street Journal column urging the United States to ban cryptocurrency outright.

**The central criticism:** Even if you think Bitcoin is a bubble, this language does not meet any standard of rational analysis. Munger never seriously examined the underlying logic of blockchain, potential applications of smart contracts, or decentralized finance's potential to expand access to financial services. His rejection was blanket, more like an older person's reflexive aversion to new technology.

### Controversy 4: The Munger Hall dormitory project

[Wikipedia / The Real Deal / Santa Barbara Independent / Arch Paper] → For details, see the section on the desire for control above. Additional facts:
- 1.68 million square feet, 11 stories, and 4,500 residents, with no daylight in most rooms.
- Compared to a prison and a Soviet hotel.
- At 97, Munger maintained that windowless rooms were “quite endurable, especially with good ventilation.”
- Residents complained about the existing Munger residence at the University of Michigan, which was also designed with windowless rooms.
- UCSB ultimately canceled the project in 2023 and switched to a conventional design.

### Controversy 5: His comments during the 2008 financial crisis

[Yahoo Finance / Wikipedia] → During the 2008 financial crisis, Munger told ordinary people to “suck it in and cope.” Coming from someone whose wealth already amounted to billions, the remark showed a serious lack of empathy.

### Controversy 6: Daily Journal's accounting

[Hedge Fund Alpha] → Activist investor Buxton Helmsley wrote to Daily Journal's board alleging accounting violations. The allegation was that software development costs were expensed when they should have been capitalized as assets. This practice occurred while Munger chaired the company.

### Controversy 7: Refusal to sign the Giving Pledge

[Inside Philanthropy / Chronicle of Philanthropy] → Munger gave away about $550 million over his lifetime and had a net worth of $2.6 billion at his death. But he refused to sign the Giving Pledge launched by Buffett and Gates, saying he had already given more than half his wealth to his children. Critics saw a tension between his public philosophy and transferring wealth to his family rather than to society, given how often he spoke of rationality and morality.

## III. Comparisons with peers

### Munger vs. Soros
| Dimension | Munger | Soros |
|---|---|---|
| Core framework | Multidisciplinary mental models and fundamental analysis | Reflexivity theory and macroeconomic trends |
| View of markets | Markets are efficient over the long run. Look for bargains. | Markets are inherently irrational and that can be exploited. |
| Flexibility | Low. Buy and hold, dislikes selling. | High. Willing to admit mistakes and reverse course quickly. |
| Attitude toward novelty | Conservative rejection, as with crypto and AI | Comparatively open |
| Political engagement | Low-profile and conservative-leaning | Extremely active, globally liberal |
| **Key difference** | Munger thinks rational people eventually win. | Soros thinks the key is understanding irrationality. |

### Munger vs. Dalio
| Dimension | Munger | Dalio |
|---|---|---|
| Systematization | Principle-based but not quantified | Highly systematized and algorithm-driven |
| Risk management | Concentrated positions and tolerance of volatility | Diversified allocation and the All Weather strategy |
| China | Invested indirectly through Li Lu, then added Alibaba in his later years at a loss | Established funds directly in China. More deeply involved, but also criticized as too friendly to the CCP. |
| Public transparency | Relies on memorable aphorisms, does not disclose specific frameworks | Writes books, publishes principles, and makes data transparent |

### Munger vs. Peter Lynch
| Dimension | Munger | Lynch |
|---|---|---|
| Track record | Strong early partnership returns, followed by later performance reflected indirectly in BRK | 29.2% annualized over 13 years, with the fund growing from $18 million to $14 billion |
| Replicability | Very low. Requires exceptional cognitive ability. | Higher. “Invest in what you know” is comparatively easy to learn. |
| Retirement timing | Kept investing until his death at 99 | Retired at 46 to enjoy life |
| Message for ordinary investors | Either it is exceptionally good or don't do it. | Ordinary people can do well too. |
| **Key difference** | Munger was a thinker rather than a portfolio operator. His investing record was far less consequential than his intellectual influence. | |

## IV. Where followers and critics disagree

### The Munger his followers see
- Pioneer of multidisciplinary mental models.
- Advocate of the study of cognitive biases.
- A revolution in thinking through inversion.
- Life wisdom: endurance, rationality, and a long-term outlook.
- A font of memorable lines at Berkshire and Daily Journal's annual meetings.

### The Munger his critics see
- **Selective rationality:** Rational in fields he mastered but highly emotional in fields he did not understand, such as technology and cryptocurrency.
- **More performer than practitioner:** He was more famous for what he said than what he did. His investment record, especially at Daily Journal, was not outstanding, and Alibaba was a major late-career mistake.
- **A blind spot about privilege:** How persuasive is advice to endure and defer gratification from someone born into a family of lawyers and judges, educated at Harvard Law School, and never worried about basic survival?
- **A closed-minded old man:** A 99-year-old dismissing 21st-century technological revolutions such as crypto and AI with “I don't like it” is not wisdom, critics say, but cognitive aging.
- **Cult of personality:** The annual meeting drew 40,000 people. Munger himself said, “You all need a new cult hero.” Followers treated his remarks as scripture and ignored his actual mistakes.

### The core disagreement: Munger's ideas vs. Munger's practice
Followers primarily consume Munger's intellectual output: speeches, aphorisms, and mental models. Critics focus more on his actual decisions. Someone can be an outstanding thinker and a poor investor in later life without contradiction.

## V. Assessments and reflections after Munger's death on November 28, 2023

### The mainstream narrative, in more than 90% of coverage
- Great investment thinker.
- Buffett's most important partner.
- Popularizer of multidisciplinary thinking.
- An example of resilience after adversity, including the death of a child, loss of sight, and divorce.

### Less common but important critical perspectives

1. **City Journal** → Acknowledged that Munger's strategy had not outperformed the market in the preceding decade and that his value-investing method looked “almost quaint” during the major technology-stock bull market. It also suggested that his success depended in part on temporary conditions such as low interest rates rather than universal principles.

2. **Medium (The Springboard)** → The headline was blunt: “Two Billion in the Bank When You Die Doesn't Make You a Success.” It suggests Munger did not fully use his potential and influence.

3. **DL News** → Characterized Munger's cryptocurrency stance as “shrill” and political rather than rational analysis.

4. **Concerns about Daily Journal's future** → The company stated in a securities filing that it did not expect future portfolio performance to match the performance achieved during Munger's lifetime. But given his late-career Alibaba mistake, the concern may be less about losing his wisdom than about losing the halo of his reputation.

5. **The end of Munger Hall** → Three months before Munger died, UCSB formally abandoned his proposed dormitory design. In some ways, the project's demise symbolized that a billionaire's will is not necessarily right.

---

## VI. Summary: Munger's core blind spots

1. **Technology:** He systematically missed technology investments such as Google and Amazon and took an extremely negative stance on cryptocurrency and AI.
2. **Illusions about China:** The lens he developed through Li Lu was too optimistic. BYD's success reinforced the bias, and the Alibaba debacle exposed his underestimation of political risk.
3. **Desire for control:** Strict conditions attached to philanthropy, as with Munger Hall, and calling professional critics “idiots.”
4. **Lack of empathy:** “Suck it in and cope,” the defense of windowless dorms, and downplaying wealth inequality.
5. **Selective rationality:** Extremely rational in familiar fields and extremely emotional in unfamiliar ones, contrary to his own account of cognitive biases.
6. **The gap between thinker and investor:** His reputation as a producer of ideas far exceeded his actual investment track record.
7. **The cult problem:** Followers' admiration made rational criticism difficult, ironically creating the very authority bias he opposed.
