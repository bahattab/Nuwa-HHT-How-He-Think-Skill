## 1
I’m speaking from a Munger perspective inferred from public remarks. These are not his own views.

On those terms, don’t go.

Invert first. How do you guarantee failure? First, take 15% with no clarity on vesting, dilution, or exit. Second, 3,000 paying users sounds impressive, but I don’t know what they pay, how many renew, or what it costs to acquire them. Three thousand paying 9 a month and three thousand paying 3,000 a year are two different businesses. Third, treat savings as venture capital. That 1.5 million isn’t ammunition. It’s your mortgage cushion.

The arithmetic is simple. You give up 560,000 a year and still owe a 3 million mortgage. The savings won’t last many years. Your wife’s anxiety is reasonable. Not objecting and feeling at ease are different. Don’t take silence for consent.

AI tools are outside my circle of competence. I only know that building one is getting easier, so the prototype is no moat. Distribution and trust are. Your former colleague already has a prototype and needs you. You’re selling your most valuable asset. Fifteen percent is too cheap.

My prescription: negotiate before deciding. Ask for more equity on a four-year vesting schedule. Get real revenue, renewal, and acquisition-cost data. Put in writing who contributes when the cash runs out. Set your own stop: use no more than 400,000 of savings and go back if after twelve months there is no clear growth. A 32-year-old product manager from a big tech company can go back. The one good thing here is the limited downside.

## 2
Don’t split it by who thinks they contributed most. Paying the loudest people the most rewards complaints.

There are already grievances. That means the rules aren’t clear. Establish the rules before discussing numbers. Make the rules public to the team and keep amounts private.

Split the million this way: divide 500,000 equally among twelve, about 42,000 each. That is the “you’re on this boat” money. Distribute the other 500,000 by verifiable contribution: revenue, delivery, customer retention. Impressions don’t count. Give the two key people 100,000 each. The other ten split 300,000, an average of 30,000. The result is about 140,000 for each key person and about 70,000 for the others. Twice as much is defensible. Three times as much will cause trouble.

The two want recognition as well as this year’s bonus. A bonus won’t buy it for long. Give them something like ownership: profit-sharing or long-term incentives that vest over time. That is how you retain key people.

One final point. Set the rules yourself. Don’t hold a vote. Compensation isn’t a question of democracy.

## 4
My answer to both is the same: don’t borrow from the future.

For financing, I want long-term fixed-rate loans. Short-term loans depend on the bank being willing to renew at maturity. The bank is least willing precisely when you need it most. That killed people in 2008. Cheap short-term debt is rat poison. It tastes sweet going down. I’ll take a little less return. You have to survive before you can compound.

On the property side, landlords who spend too little are consuming their future. Leave a roof leaking and old pipes in place, and you will pay back twice what you saved on paper as tenants leave, reputation deteriorates, and rents stagnate. Rental apartments are a long-term business. The older the property gets, the more good tenants leave. I want to fix it up after buying so good tenants want to stay.

As for that neighbor who knocked at seventeen: a seventeen-year-old willing to knock on a stranger’s door has nerve and the patience to be turned down. I look at people more than spreadsheets. I’ll bet on that.

One note: I’m inferring the decisions from my principles, not recounting the actual details.

## 5
It was a cheap, neglected deal we could hold for a long time. No clever arbitrage.

The reasoning was plain. The five trading houses are diversified commodities and investment businesses in energy, metals, food, infrastructure, and other sectors. They are a little like smaller Japanese Berkshires. Valuations were low, dividends respectable, and they were buying back shares. Better still was the cost of funds: yen borrowing at nearly zero interest. Borrow cheap, buy cheap assets, and sit tight.

Why couldn’t others do it? First, scale. Berkshire could buy billions of dollars of shares at once. Others either couldn’t afford it or would move the price by buying. Second, reputation. The Japanese companies were comfortable because Berkshire pledged to stay below a given stake and not interfere. Third, patience. Most funds have to produce a quarterly report card and can’t wait.

Warren made this deal, not me. I don’t know the Japanese market well, so I’ll stop there.

What went wrong was habit, not intelligence. We turned the circle of competence into a wall. We were comfortable inside it and stopped looking at good things outside. We created that problem ourselves and knew it for a long time without acting.

Then there is size. With enough money, even excellent small opportunities aren’t worth the time. That’s a cost of success.

## 7
It’s outside my circle of competence, but I’ll say a few things.

Coding is easier, so getting a product built is no longer rare. Don’t expect anything that gets cheaper to be a moat. When everyone can make a product, winners have something else: people willing to pay, trust, distribution, or a narrow but deep need other people won’t bother serving.

Invert it. How do you guarantee failure? Build something nobody will pay for. Take on a platform head-on. Assume coding with AI is an advantage when a million other people can do the same. Put all your savings in.

Is it worth doing? Yes, if the downside is limited. Costs are low, you can spare the time, and if it fails you learn. Working alone with no funding pressure or partner disputes puts you in better shape than most founders. But you must also find out who will pay.
