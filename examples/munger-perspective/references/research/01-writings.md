## Verification and corrections

1. **Origin of the 25 tendencies.** The earlier notes dated the list of 25 to the 1995 Harvard talk. The earlier SKILL instead called it a full 2003 version. The three talks in the 1990s were improvised from outlines, and the list of twenty-five standard causes took shape in the 2005 rewrite. He wrote: "The final revision was made from memory over about fifty hours by a man eighty-one years old, who never took a course in psychology and has read none of it, except one book on developmental psychology, for nearly fifteen years." He also hoped professors of psychology would join him in "(1) making heavy use of inversion; (2) driving for a complete description of the psychological system so that it works better as a checklist; and (3) especially emphasizing effects from combinations of psychological tendencies."
2. **“Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome.”** The old notes and SKILL used the line as a direct quotation two or three times. No primary source was located this time. It was absent from the talks, annual meetings, and *Almanack* checked, and online attributions gave no occasion. The verified statement from the talk is: "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives." The FedEx night-shift example is also documented. The new version drops the unverified line as a verbatim quote.
3. **“The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting.”** No primary source was located for this one either. The idea can be traced to Livermore's account in *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* (1923): "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It always was my sitting." Do not use the unverified wording as a Munger quotation.
4. **“Darwin probably changed my life.”** No source was located, so remove it. Munger's reference to Darwin's habit of specifically seeking contrary evidence is verifiable in the 2007 USC law school address.
5. **The four prescriptions in the 1986 speech.** The old SKILL called these the four ways to guarantee misery. The actual structure opens with Johnny Carson's three prescriptions (substance abuse, envy, and resentment), then adds Munger's own four: be unreliable, learn only from firsthand experience and ignore other people's lessons, stay down after one or two setbacks, and ignore the countryman's advice ("I wish I knew where I was going to die, and then I'd never go there"), meaning to refuse to invert.
6. **His examples of incentives concern real episodes he described.** FedEx paid its night shift by the hour and was never able to finish transferring packages. Paying by the shift and letting workers leave when the job was done solved the problem. At Xerox, early sales of new machines lagged sales of old ones because commissions favored the older equipment (Joe Wilson). The source is the talk on human misjudgment.
7. **Original Lollapalooza examples.** Tupperware parties (reciprocation, commitment and consistency, and social proof), and open-outcry auctions (social proof, deprival superreaction, and commitment). "three, four, five of these things work together and it turns human brains into mush".
8. The formulation about being entitled to an opinion ("I never allow myself to have an opinion on anything that I don't know the other side's argument better than they do") is associated with the language of his 2007 USC law school speech. The old notes named a Daily Journal annual meeting. The precise occasion is uncertain, while the thrust of his words is clear.

## Differences from prior knowledge

- Correction: No primary source was found for “Show me the incentive…,” the most widely circulated alleged Munger quote.
- Correction: The 25 tendencies came from the 2005 rewrite, written from memory at 81. The list did not come from the 1995 talk. Munger said he had never taken a psychology class and had read almost no psychology books in nearly fifteen years.
- Refinement: The 1986 talk lists Carson’s three points plus Munger’s four. The venue was a secondary school.
- Addition: He wrote open letters and opinion pieces as well as giving talks. The 1989 resignation from the savings and loan association and the 2023 WSJ piece urging a crypto ban were polemics aimed at specific audiences and pressing for specific action.
