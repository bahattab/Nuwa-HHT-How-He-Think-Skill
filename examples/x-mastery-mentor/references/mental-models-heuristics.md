## Core Mental Models (6)

### Model 1: The Lean Validation Flywheel

**In one sentence**: Publish the smallest piece of content that can test your idea. Expand it if it works, then use that expanded piece to generate new ideas.

**How it works**:
```
Tweet (test the idea)
  ↓ Good results?
Thread (explore in depth)
  ↓ Good results?
Newsletter/Blog (long-form asset)
  ↓ Good results?
Video/course/product (monetize)
  ↑ Feed new ideas back ←──────┘
```

**Sources**: Cole/Bush (Lean Writing), Sahil (validated all 225+ threads first), Hormozi (tweet → video pipeline), and Welsh (Content OS). Four distinct approaches converged independently on the same pattern.

**Application**: Before writing long-form content, ask “Have I tested this idea in a tweet?”

**Limitations**: A low-frequency, high-quality approach like Karpathy's doesn't depend on this flywheel. It relies on personal authority and on content that's hard to find elsewhere. The flywheel is best suited to someone still building an audience, not an established authority with a million followers.

### Model 2: Attention Engineering

**In one sentence**: The first 2 lines make or break a piece of content. You can deliberately engineer a better hook.

**Core formula**:

Hook quality = curiosity gap × credibility × specificity

- **Curiosity gap** (Cole): Reveal the beginning and the ending, but hold back the middle to give readers a reason to click.
- **Credibility**: Use numbers, names, and time anchors (“I studied 1,000 accounts…”).
- **Specificity**: Add constraints until the claim is “uncomfortably specific” (Cole's Headline Checklist).

**Applying Hormozi's Value Equation to a hook**:
```
Hook value = (desired outcome × credibility) / (time cost × effort)
```
The bigger the numerator and the smaller the denominator, the harder the hook is to resist.

**Algorithmic rationale**: X's engagement velocity mechanism treats engagement speed in the first 15–30 minutes as a signal for whether a post reaches a wider audience or stalls. The hook shapes how the post performs in that window.

**Operating rules**:
- Spend 50% of creation time on the hook. Write 10–15 versions, then pick the best (Cole).
- The headline must answer 3 questions. Who is it for? What is it about? Why should someone read it?
- See `writing-workshop.md > Hook Improvement Examples (Before → After)` for comparisons.

**Limitations**: Over-optimizing the hook can turn it into clickbait. The content has to deliver what the hook promises, or it will erode trust over time.

### Model 3: Category Creation

**In one sentence**: Instead of squeezing into an existing niche, create a category that only you occupy.

**Three levels of development**:

| Level | Strategy | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Beginner | Niche down | “AI tool reviews” |
| Intermediate | Combine an Interest Stack | “AI + indie development + product thinking” |
| Advanced | Create a category | Coin a new term and redefine the field |

**Cole's Snow Leopard theory**: Don't be a lion competing to be the strongest in an existing category. Be a snow leopard that occupies a unique position in a field few others inhabit.

**Koe's Niche of One**:
- Create a niche instead of looking for one.
- Formula: Broad brand (share a range of interests) + narrow product (address a specific problem).
- Interest Stack: Combine interests to create a distinctive perspective (fitness + philosophy + business + lifestyle).

**Languaging (the art of naming)**: Give your distinctive method its own name. Two words can change how people see a category (car → electric car). Ship 30 for 30 is itself an example of languaging.

**Tequila Test (category test)**:
1. List all the conventional advice on your topic.
2. Cross all of it out.
3. Write about what's left. If nothing remains to write about, you don't yet have a genuinely distinct viewpoint.

**Application**: Use this when your positioning is unclear, your work feels interchangeable with others', or your niche feels overcrowded.

**Limitations**: Creating a category takes time and deep expertise. If you're starting from scratch, you may first need to build an audience in an existing category.

### Model 4: Lead with Value

**In one sentence**: Give away the secrets and sell the implementation. Every piece of content should deliver something valuable.

**Hormozi's central insight**: Only 1% will do it themselves, while 99% are willing to pay someone else to do it for them. Give away high-value content → show that you have a solution → build trust and reciprocity → conversion follows naturally.

**Three-part content structure (Hook–Retain–Reward)**:
1. **Hook**: Grab attention immediately (surprise, a question, or a bold promise).
2. **Retain**: Keep delivering value (story + open loops + no filler).
3. **Reward**: Deliver more than you promised (practical next steps readers can act on).

**Welsh's education-first approach**: Establish authority by teaching first, then guide readers toward a paid offer. The key practice in his growth of 44K followers in 18 weeks was “posting educational tips in the afternoon.”

**Sahil's Feynman-style test**: If you can't explain a complex idea in the simplest terms, you haven't understood it yet. Writing is a way to practice the Feynman technique in public.

**Application**: Before writing any piece, ask “What can the reader do immediately after reading this?” If the answer is “nothing,” rewrite it.

**Limitations**: Useful advice alone won't build a personal connection. Mix in personal stories and opinions (Dickie Bush's 75/25 rule: 75% broad content to attract new readers and 25% in-depth content to retain them).

### Model 5: Build in Public

**In one sentence**: Turn your process into content and make the audience feel invested in it.

**Two variants**:

**Build in Public (levelsio)**:
- Share revenue (MRR screenshots), the process (feature iterations), and failures (97% of projects fail).
- Core mechanism: When people watch you go from zero to $100K MRR, they develop an “investor mindset.” They want you to succeed and spread the word themselves.
- What to share: MRR milestones, feature releases, failure retrospectives, tech-stack decisions, and user feedback.
- What not to share: Exact customer acquisition costs, customers' personal information, or key implementation details.

**Learn in Public (swyx)**:
- Make your learning visible. Write blog posts and tutorials, and ask and answer questions in forums to produce “learning exhaust.”
- **Pick Up What They Put Down**: When a prominent creator releases something new, write a review, analysis, or tutorial and tag them. They're likely to repost it because “I can repost people praising my work all day.”
- You don't have to invent something new. You have to explain what others have invented clearly.

**Application**: A central differentiation strategy in the AI and tech niche. Suited to indie developers, creators building products, and people learning technical skills.

**Limitations**: You have to be doing something. A creator who only comments on others' work has nothing to build in public. It also takes resilience, because failures are public too.

### Model 6: Systematic Compounding

**In one sentence**: Replace dependence on inspiration with templates and systems so you can produce content consistently.

**Welsh's Content OS**:
1. **Curate**: Collect inspiration and high-performing content.
2. **Templatize**: Extract templates from successful content structures.
3. **Rapid Create**: Use templates and source material to produce 10–20 pieces per hour.
4. **Distribute**: Publish across platforms using automation tools.

**Koe's 2 Hour Writer**:
- 1 hour walking to gather ideas + 1 hour writing and editing.
- Idea Museum: An organized library of material.
- Quick-reference writing formats: Listicle / short post (personal redefinition or an uncomfortable truth) / PSB story arc.

**Content reuse flywheel**:
```
Newsletter (1–2 long pieces per week)
  ├── Extract 5–7 short posts → Twitter/X
  ├── Screenshots of Twitter posts → Instagram/LinkedIn
  ├── Read the newsletter aloud → YouTube
  └── High-engagement tweets → Develop into topics for the next newsletter
```

**Sahil's Notion board**: Raw idea → Up next → In progress → Finished, unpublished → Published. Five columns keep the content pipeline full.

**Application**: Use it when you're struggling to write, running out of ideas, or publishing inconsistently. A system helps you produce solid work even on off days.

**Limitations**: Too much systematization makes content mechanical. Leave 20–30% unscheduled for inspiration and spontaneous responses, especially to fast-moving AI stories.

## Decision Heuristics (10)

### 1. Tweet Before Writing Long-Form ← Apply Model 1
Have a long-form idea? Test it in a tweet first. “Twitter is an idea refinery, not a broadcast channel” (Bush).
- **Trigger**: When you're about to write a thread, a newsletter, or a video script.
- **Action**: Post a tweet to test the core idea. Expand it if it performs well.

### 2. Spend 50% of Creation Time on the Hook ← Apply Model 2
Write 10–15 versions and choose the best. The headline must answer three questions. Who is it for? What is it about? Why read it? See “Hook Improvement Examples.”
- **Trigger**: When you start writing any piece of content.

### 3. Conversation Beats Everything
Algorithm weights: reply that starts a conversation 150× > reply 27× > bookmark 20× > repost 2× > like 1×. A conversation that draws a reply from the author is worth more than 150 likes.
- **Trigger**: When thinking about your engagement strategy.
- **Action**: Write something that invites replies (a question, a controversial take, or a request for feedback), and reply actively to every comment.

### 4. The 1/3/1 Rhythm
1 sentence for the hook + 3 sentences to develop the idea + 1 sentence for the transition. This makes the content easy to skim. Single-sentence lines work like checkpoints, giving readers small dopamine hits.
- **Trigger**: When writing anything longer than 3 sentences.
- **Variants**: 1/4/1, 1/5/1, 1/2/5/2/1.

### 5. The Super Bowl Response (AI Niche)
A major model release is the AI niche's Super Bowl. Response timeline: 0–1 hour for a quick take → 1–6 hours for a demo → 6–24 hours for an in-depth thread → 1–7 days for a full review.
- **Trigger**: A major GPT, Claude, Gemini, or open-source model release.
- **Key**: Speed > perfection. The first person with something insightful to say is more valuable than the hundredth person with a perfect analysis.

### 6. Own Your Audience
The algorithm will change, but your email list remains yours. The ultimate goal of every tweet is to send readers to your newsletter. Twitter is the discovery engine (top of funnel). The newsletter is where you build a deeper relationship with an audience you can reach directly.
- **Trigger**: When planning your content strategy.
- **Sahil's numbers**: $70K+ per month from newsletter ads, all reinvested in growth.

### 7. The 4A Topic Matrix
One topic × 4 angles = endless topics:
- **Actionable**: Teach readers what to do (tips, guides, how-tos).
- **Analytical**: Back up your points with data (stats, trends, frameworks).
- **Aspirational**: Show readers what's possible (lessons, mistakes, habits).
- **Anthropological**: Tap into human nature (fears, failures, lies, struggles).
- **Trigger**: When you feel like there's nothing to write about.

### 8. Give Away the Secrets and Sell the Implementation ← Apply Model 4
If you're unsure whether to share something for free, share it. 99% of people won't put it into practice themselves. (Hormozi: 1 million followers in 6 months with zero advertising spend.)
- **Trigger**: When you're unsure whether to give away a method, tool, or process.

### 9. Templates Beat Inspiration
Extract templates from successful content and use them to publish faster. Cole used only 7 thread templates to write 200+ threads. Welsh uses Content OS to produce 10–20 pieces per hour.
- **Trigger**: As soon as a piece performs well, turn it into a template.
- **Cole's 7 templates**: Framework / Story / Actionable / Curation / Lessons / Mistakes / Contrarian.

### 10. Comment Sections Are Gold Mines
Leave high-quality replies under posts from large accounts to reach their audiences. Welsh reported 6,700 impressions on one reply. When Sahil was starting out, he posted threads in the replies of 50 large accounts. One repost from Chamath sparked rapid growth.
- **Trigger**: When you're still building an audience and have fewer than 10K followers.
- **Rule**: Don't write “Great post.” Write a 200–400-character reply that reads like a mini-newsletter.
