## I. Background

**Paul Graham** (born November 13, 1964) is a British-American computer scientist, writer, essayist, entrepreneur, and investor.

### Key biographical details
- **Birth**: Weymouth, Dorset, England. His family moved to Pittsburgh, USA, when he was four.
- **Education**: BA from Cornell (1986), then a master's and PhD in computer science at Harvard, then painting at RISD (Rhode Island School of Design) and the Academy of Fine Arts in Florence.
- **Viaweb** (1995–1998): Co-founded with Robert Morris. The online store-building software was written in Common Lisp. Yahoo bought it in 1998 for $49.6 million and turned it into Yahoo Store.
- **Y Combinator** (2005 onward): Founded with Trevor Blackwell, Jessica Livingston, and Robert Morris. It has invested in over 1,300 startups, including Reddit, Dropbox, Airbnb, and Stripe.
- **Arc**: Development of the new Lisp dialect was announced in 2001, and it shipped in 2008.
- **Books**: *On Lisp*, *ANSI Common Lisp*, and *Hackers & Painters*.
- **Essays**: Over 200 essays on paulgraham.com between 1998 and 2026, at a rate of one every four to eight weeks.

> Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Graham_(programmer)), [paulgraham.com/bio.html](https://paulgraham.com/bio.html) | Primary and reliable

## II. Major Book: Hackers & Painters

**Publication**: O'Reilly Media, 2004
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/hp.html

### Main arguments

1. **Programming is creative work, not science**: Hackers and painters are both makers. Graham disputes the term “computer science”: it isn't really a science, but a collection of fields grouped by historical accident.
2. **Learn through practice**: Painting is mainly learned by doing, and so is programming. Most hackers didn't learn to code in college classes. They learned by writing their own programs at thirteen.
3. **A programming language should be a pencil, not an ink pen**: It should be malleable enough to think through a program, not just express one already worked out.
4. **Develop iteratively**: A painting begins with a sketch and grows more detailed. Programming should work the same way. Sometimes the original plan turns out to be wrong.
5. **Empathy is a core skill**: Like painters, hackers must empathize and see things from the user's perspective to do great work.

> Sources: [paulgraham.com/hp.html](https://paulgraham.com/hp.html), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/41793.Hackers_Painters), [Medium analysis](https://medium.com/@edisipka/my-notes-on-paul-grahams-hackers-and-painters-why-programming-is-actually-art-9a4829117554) | Primary and secondary

## III. Six Core Essays on Writing Method

### 3.1 The Age of the Essay (2004)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/essay.html

- An essay should be an **exploration**, not a proof of a predetermined proposition.
- The five-paragraph argument taught in school distorts the nature of the essay.
- The French root “essai” means “attempt.” Montaigne invented the form.
- A good essay records the process of thinking, not merely its result.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 3.2 Writing, Briefly (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html

Graham's codified writing rules:
- Write a bad first version as quickly as possible, then rewrite it repeatedly.
- If you get stuck, tell someone what you intend to write, then write down what you said.
- Expect 80% of the essay's ideas to emerge after you start writing.
- Start by writing the first sentence.
- Read your essay aloud to catch awkward phrases and boring passages.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 3.3 Write Like You Talk (2015)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/talk.html

- Writing and speech should be connected.
- Read your article aloud and revise anything that doesn't sound conversational.
- Do that, and you're already ahead of 95% of writers.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 3.4 Write Simply (2021)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/simply.html

- “I try to write in ordinary words. … The easier the writing is to read, the more deeply readers engage with it.”
- Keep language, words, and sentences simple without reducing the depth of thought.
- Simple writing is a *choice*, not a lack of ability.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 3.5 How to Write Usefully (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/useful.html

- Useful writing = **importance × novelty × correctness × strength**.
- Of the four variables, novelty is the easiest to overlook.
- Writing isn't just transmitting information. It should change how people see something.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 3.6 Putting Ideas into Words (2022)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/words.html

- **Core claim**: Writing is thinking.
- Putting ideas in writing forces you to think more clearly.
- Many people believe their thinking is clear before they write. It isn't. Writing itself produces new understanding.
- This echoes the 2024 essay “Writes and Write-Nots.”

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### Common core of Graham's writing method

Graham's writing philosophy can be summarized as:
1. **Writing = thinking** (occurs at least five times).
2. **Simple > complex** (at least four).
3. **Conversational > formal** (at least three).
4. **Iterative writing**: draft badly first, then revise repeatedly (at least three).
5. **Exploration > argument** (at least three).

## IV. Key Essays on Startups

### 4.1 How to Start a Startup (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/start.html

Three things a startup needs to succeed:
1. Start with good people.
2. Make something customers actually want.
3. Spend as little as possible.

Other key points:
- Co-founders are to a startup what location is to real estate.
- Launch quickly. “You haven't really started working until you've launched.”
- Delighting a few people beats moderately pleasing many.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.2 Do Things that Don't Scale (2013)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

- **Main idea**: Early founders should embrace manual, labor-intensive work even when it won't scale.
- The most common example is recruiting users manually.
- “If you can find someone with a problem and solve it manually, do it. That's far better than automating something nobody needs.”
- Ten customers plus 10% growth weekly: exponential growth takes care of the small starting base.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.3 Startup = Growth (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/growth.html

- **Growth defines a startup**, not youth or small size.
- At YC, a good growth rate is 5–7% a week, and 10% is excellent.
- $1,000 per month plus 1% weekly growth becomes $7,900 per month after four years.
- $1,000 per month plus 5% weekly growth becomes $25 million per month after four years.
- Compounding small percentages produces radically different outcomes.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.4 Default Alive or Default Dead? (2015)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/aord.html

- Founders should know whether their company is default alive or default dead.
- The calculation needs four figures: current spending, revenue, growth rate, and cash on hand.
- Default-alive companies have more negotiating leverage.
- **Hiring too fast is the leading killer of startups after funding**.
- The “fatal pinch” is being default dead, growing slowly, and having no time to fix it.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.5 Frighteningly Ambitious Startup Ideas (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/ambitious.html

- The most ambitious startup ideas are frightening because they're genuinely hard.
- The essay lists several such directions.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.6 Schlep Blindness (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html

- “Schlep blindness”: People miss great startup ideas because they involve schleps, a Yiddish term for tedious, unpleasant work.
- Many of the best opportunities are missed because people instinctively avoid the dirty work.
- Stripe's Collison brothers are the counterexample: they took on payments, the area nobody wanted to touch.

> Sources: Primary + [LinkedIn discussion](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/the-startup-archive_alexandr-wang-on-why-paul-grahams-schlep-activity-7369036483380314112-nd2v) | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.7 How to Get Startup Ideas (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html

- The best ideas share three traits: founders themselves want them, can build them, and few others realize they're worth doing.
- “The way to succeed isn't to become a startup expert, but an expert on your users and the problem.”

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 4.8 Founder Mode (September 2024)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html

- Inspired by Airbnb co-founder Brian Chesky's talk at a YC event.
- **Two management modes**: founder mode versus professional manager mode.
- Silicon Valley convention says growing companies must switch to manager mode. Graham says that's wrong.
- Chesky found that “hire people and let go” was disastrous for Airbnb.
- Chesky studied how Steve Jobs ran Apple and shifted to founder mode, with a major improvement.
- Founders should know the product's details as deeply as a chief product officer.
- **Graham's most viral essay of 2024**, provoking debate throughout technology circles.

> Sources: Primary + [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/09/01/paul-graham-founder-mode-silicon-valley-conventional-wisdom-manager-mode/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founder_mode) | Reliability: ★★★★★

## V. Essays on Life Philosophy and Cognition

### 5.1 How to Do Great Work (2023)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html

A four-step framework:
1. Choose a field you're interested in and have an aptitude for.
2. Learn enough to reach the frontier of knowledge.
3. Notice gaps, patterns, and anomalies others miss.
4. Explore the most promising gaps.

Key claims:
- **Curiosity is the key to doing great work**. It helps choose the field, reach its frontier, spot gaps, and drive exploration.
- The factors in great work, in the mathematical sense, are ability, interest, effort, and luck.
- “A page a day sounds little, but keep it up and it becomes a book in a year.” This shows the accumulated effect of consistency.
- Great work means doing something important well enough to expand people's sense of what's possible.
- Follow true interests rather than prestige.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.2 Superlinear Returns (2023)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/superlinear.html

- **Superlinear returns**: Double the effort and the output may quadruple or more.
- Two drivers: **exponential growth (compounding)** and **threshold effects (winner takes all)**.
- Science has the highest superlinear returns because it combines learning, thresholds, and discoveries.
- The obvious way to tap these returns is to work exceptionally well. Far along the curve, extra effort is a bargain and competition is thinner.
- **Always be learning**. If you aren't learning, you may not be on a path to superlinear returns.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.3 Life is Short (2016)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/vb.html

Three guidelines for action:
1. **Ruthlessly prune the bullshit**: unnecessary meetings, pointless arguments, bureaucracy, and pretension.
2. **Don't wait**: Climb the mountain, write the book, or go see your mother.
3. **Savor the time you have**.

Personal notes:
- “After my mother died, I wished I'd spent more time with her. I'd lived as if she'd always be there.”
- Pointless obligations enter your life in two ways: you're forced into them or tricked into accepting them.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.4 The Bus Ticket Theory of Genius (2019)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/genius.html

- Genius = **a disinterested obsession with something that matters**.
- “Disinterested” is essential. You pursue the thing for its own sake, not to impress people or become rich.
- Paths to new ideas often look unpromising. If they looked promising, someone else would already have explored them.
- Signs: you're creating rather than consuming, what interests you is difficult, and the difficulty comes more easily to you than to others.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.5 Keep Your Identity Small (2009)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/identity.html

- “The more labels you give yourself, the dumber they make you.”
- Once a topic becomes part of your identity, you can't think rationally about it.
- Religion and politics prompt the fiercest arguments not because they're special, but because people make them part of their identities.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.6 What You Can't Say (2004)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/say.html

- Every age has beliefs people consider right but that are absurd. It's unlikely ours is the first to get everything right.
- **Test**: Is there any opinion you wouldn't dare express to peers? If not, it probably isn't a coincidence. You may simply think what others have told you to think.
- To find hidden taboos, see what speech gets people in trouble, identify labels used to silence them, and compare cultures and eras.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.7 How to Think for Yourself (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/think.html

Three components of independent thinking:
1. Fastidiousness about truth.
2. Resistance to being told what to think.
3. Curiosity.

“If your answers show that everything you believe is what you're supposed to believe, it probably isn't a coincidence.”

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.8 The Four Quadrants of Conformism (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/conformism.html

Four types of people:
1. **Aggressively conventional-minded**.
2. **Passively conventional-minded**.
3. **Passively independent-minded**.
4. **Aggressively independent-minded**.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.9 What You'll Wish You'd Known (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/hs.html

- An undelivered commencement speech for high school students.
- Don't panic about finding a life goal. Most successful people discover theirs along the way.
- **“Stay upwind”**: Like a glider, at each stage, do the most interesting thing that leaves you with the most options later.
- Curiosity doesn't lie. It knows better than you what is worth your attention.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.10 Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule (2009)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html

- **Two schedules**: Managers divide the day into hours. Makers work in blocks of at least half a day.
- A single meeting can ruin a maker's whole afternoon by splitting it in two segments too small for hard work.
- Managers usually hold power and make everyone work to their rhythm.
- Graham's solution: Cluster all meetings at the end of the workday as office hours.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

### 5.11 Mean People Fail (2014)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/mean.html

- Few of the most successful people Graham knows are mean.
- Meanness makes you stupid. You can't do your best work while fighting.
- Mean founders don't attract the best talent.
- Great work needs to be driven by benevolence.
- Historically, much success came in zero-sum games, where meanness can be an advantage. Startups aren't one of those games.

**Controversy**: Critics say that successful founders such as Jobs, Zuckerberg, and Bezos had a mean side. Graham's argument may be overly idealistic.

> Sources: Primary + [Inc. rebuttal](https://www.inc.com/jeff-bercovici/paul-graham-mean-people-fail.html) | Reliability: ★★★★☆ (contested)

### 5.12 The Submarine (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/submarine.html

- PR firms lurk beneath the news like a huge, quiet submarine.
- More than half of news outside politics, crime, and disasters may originate in PR.
- A PR firm feeds the same story to multiple outlets at once. Readers think it's a trend when it's manufactured.
- Top journalists are vulnerable to vanity, not laziness.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

## VI. Technology and Programming Essays

### 6.1 Beating the Averages (2003)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/avg.html

- **The Blub Paradox**: A hypothetical Blub programmer looks down and sees weaker languages lacking features. Looking up, he can't recognize that a language is more powerful. He sees only strange languages with useless additions.
- “The only programmers who can clearly see differences in language power are those who understand the most powerful one.”
- Writing Viaweb in Lisp was a crucial competitive advantage. It let the team ship features ahead of competitors.

> Source: Primary | Reliability: ★★★★★

## VII. Recent Essays (2024–2025 and After)

### 7.1 Writes and Write-Nots (2024/2025)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/writes.html

- Predicts that the AI era will divide people into “writes” and “write-nots.”
- “Not good, average, and non-writers. Just good writers and people who can't write.”
- **Writing is thinking**. Skipping the skill of writing also skips learning to think clearly.
- Analogy: Before industrialization, most jobs kept people physically strong. Today you need to exercise if you want to be strong. Writing is similar. “There will still be smart people, but only those who choose to be smart.”
- **“A world divided into writes and write-nots is more dangerous than it sounds. It will be a world of thinks and think-nots.”**

## XI. Graham's Writing-Style DNA

Based on secondary analyses and a direct reading of Graham's essays:

### Syntax
- Prefers **short sentences and short words**, even for sophisticated ideas.
- About 70% of essays contain “example.” An abstract idea is typically followed within a sentence or two by a well-chosen example.
- Often uses the second-person “you,” addressing the reader directly.
- Almost no jargon. He conveys unusual ideas in ordinary words.

### Structure
- Avoids the five-paragraph form in favor of **free exploration in essay form**.
- Usually starts from an observation or question and gradually develops it.
- Uses turns such as “incidentally,” “in fact,” and “it turns out.”
- Endings are often open rather than capped with a summary.

### Rhetoric
- **Analogies and metaphors** are his most common tools: “programming is like painting,” “startups are like gliders,” and “ideas are like moral fashions.”
- Rhetorical questions: “If all your beliefs are the ones you're supposed to hold, can that be a coincidence?”
- Lists: He often inserts a key list midway through an essay.
- Self-correction: “I may be wrong, but...” and “There may be exceptions...”

### Thinking
- **From particular to general**: Begin with a concrete story or case and extract a universal principle.
- **Inversion**: Often starts by asking what's wrong, then works out what's right.
- **Analogies across domains**: Moves frequently among painting, programming, startups, and writing.
- **Honesty about uncertainty**: The essays repeatedly admit uncertainty and the possibility of error.

## XII. Contradictions and Controversies

### Contradiction 1: Mean People Fail versus reality
- **Graham's position**: Mean people fail in startups.
- **Counterexamples**: Jobs, Zuckerberg, and Bezos are widely seen as having a mean side, but they were extremely successful.
- **Possible reconciliation**: Graham may mean sheer meanness without competence, rather than being demanding.

### Contradiction 2: Founder Mode versus earlier advice
- **Graham in 2024**: Founders should be deeply involved in operating details.
- **Earlier Graham**: Several essays advised founders to focus on what matters most and delegate.
- **Possible explanation**: His thinking evolved, and Founder Mode corrects his earlier advice on delegation.

### Contradiction 3: Dispute over economic inequality
- Graham defended economic inequality as a by-product of innovation.
- This prompted critical articles from Quartz and other outlets.
- The stance is in tension with some of his arguments about benevolence.

### Contradiction 4: AI optimism versus AI concern
- **Optimism**: AI is the missing piece in many puzzles and an important technology.
- **Concern**: AI will divide people into “writes and write-nots” and “thinks and think-nots.”
- The two aren't wholly contradictory, but show that his attitude toward AI is complex.

## XIV. Research Summary

### Main findings

1. **Paul Graham is one of the most influential contemporary essayists**, with over 200 essays covering entrepreneurship, writing, programming, and life philosophy.

2. **His ideas are highly consistent and interconnected**: Curiosity leads to independent thinking, writing as thinking, great work, and superlinear returns. Together they form an intellectual system.

3. **His style is the best demonstration of his method**: Use the simplest words for the deepest ideas, and distill general principles from firsthand experience with Viaweb and YC.

4. **The two most influential essays of 2024**: Founder Mode redefines company management, and Writes and Write-Nots anticipates AI's effects on human thinking.

5. **He coined terms now common in Silicon Valley**: ramen profitable, schlep blindness, do things that don't scale, founder mode, and the Blub paradox, among others.

6. **His main tension** lies between idealism (mean people fail, benevolence as a driving force) and reality. But he usually admits he may be wrong.

### Research limitations

- Full text on paulgraham.com wasn't directly accessible. Only search summaries and quotations were available.
- Information about 2025–2026 essays is limited, with possible omissions.
- The book list is based on secondary compilations and may be incomplete.
- The exact dates of some essays still need confirmation.
