# Paul Graham: Patterns of expression

> Research date: 2026-04-05
> Research objective: Identify Graham's linguistic fingerprints in Twitter/X posts, essays, and controversial positions to inform the skill's expression layer.

---

## 1. Style overview: Precision disguised as simplicity

Graham's writing is often described as writing the way one speaks. Several analysts argue that this impression is carefully constructed.

**Central tension.** The surface is spare and the structure underneath is precise.

- Sasha Chapin argues that Graham writes with complexity and subtlety. Because the complexity lies outside his vocabulary, it is easy to miss without close reading. [Sasha Chapin, Substack](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer)
- Henry Oliver compares his style with the realism of theater or film. It simulates real conversation but is carefully structured. He combines simple units to produce an almost perfect rhythm. [Henry Oliver, Common Reader](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric)

**Quantifiable characteristics**

- About 70% of the essays contain “for example,” suggesting that a concrete example follows an abstract point within one or two sentences.
- More than 200 essays over more than 25 years.
- He favors simple words with Germanic roots, but empirical analysis finds no significant difference in the share of Latinate words compared with peers such as Sam Altman. [Arjun Panickssery, Substack](https://arjunpanickssery.substack.com/p/germanic-vs-latinate-words-in-writing)

## 2. Sentence patterns and frequent expressions

### 2.1 Characteristic sentence patterns

| Pattern | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **"The way to X is to Y"** | "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems." | Reveal a counterintuitive method |
| **"Most people don't realize..."** | "Most people dislike uncertainty" | Introduce a widespread blind spot |
| **"It turns out..."** | "It turns out to be very useful to work on what interests you the most" | Reveal an unexpected finding |
| **"X is like Y"** | "Startups are as unnatural as skiing" / a programming language "should be a pencil, not a pen" | Explain by analogy |
| **"If you X, you Y"** | "If you're in a job that feels safe, you are not going to get exceptional" | Show conditional causation |
| **"The [adj]-est [noun] are..."** | "The statements that make people maddest are those they worry might be true" | Push a point to its extreme |
| **"There's a [noun] that..."** | "There's a third ingredient that's not as well understood" | Introduce a neglected factor |
| **"I think" / "I suspect"** | "I suspect few housing projects in the US were designed by architects who expected to live in them" | Combine a modest qualification with a sharp point |
| **Balanced "[X] and [Y]"** | "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" | Create rhythm through parallelism |

### 2.2 Essay openings

Graham rarely opens with a definition or quotation. He uses these approaches instead.

1. **Start with a personal anecdote.** "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" / "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years"
2. **State the conventional view and make a turn.** "Everyone knows that to do great work you need both natural ability and determination. But there's a third ingredient that's not as well understood"
3. **State a bold claim directly.** Go straight to the subject without a preamble.
4. **Ask and answer a question (antipophora).** Pose a question and develop an exploratory answer.

### 2.3 Frequent vocabulary

**Preferred words**

- Plain verbs: think, know, want, need, make, start, try, seem, work
- Qualifiers: probably, usually, most, roughly, fairly, somewhat
- Conversational connectors: but, so, and, though, because
- Metaphorical words: seed, path, landscape, machine, lever

**Words he explicitly dislikes**

- "delve." On Twitter in 2024 he called it a ChatGPT tell, sparking the “delve” controversy.
- "burgeoning." He likewise treats it as a sign of stilted or AI-like prose.
- Academic-sounding words, such as methodology, utilize, and facilitate.
- Corporate jargon, such as synergy, leverage as a verb, and paradigm shift.

## 3. Rhetorical techniques

### 3.1 Parallelism

Graham frequently balances the two halves of a sentence to create rhythm.

- Mirrored structures like "had" / "having" and "sold" / "suddenly."
- Tricolon, deliberately extending the last element to add weight, as in "natural ability, determination, and an obsessive interest in a particular topic".

[Henry Oliver's analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric)

### 3.2 Self-riffing

After introducing a word in a paragraph, Graham returns to it in later sentences to make language itself bridge the argument. Sasha Chapin describes the transitions between points as exceptionally smooth.

[Sasha Chapin's analysis](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer)

### 3.3 Strategic use of sophisticated words

Graham normally sticks to simple words. He occasionally inserts a sophisticated expression for impact. Chapin gives "spectral signature of a hater" as an example. Because unusual language is rare, it feels satisfying, emphasizes the judgment, and catches attention.

### 3.4 Questions and answers (antipophora)

His essays often advance through questions he then answers, like a Shakespearean soliloquy. He shows each stage of thought rather than compressing the problem into one sentence.

### 3.5 Conversational tone

Specific techniques include:

- Mixing contractions and full forms, with "I'm" and "they are" appearing in one paragraph to mimic variation in real speech.
- Conversational emphasis, such as "very specific."
- Successive questions.
- Mixing high and low registers, combining an intellectual idea with a colloquial expression.

## 4. Style on Twitter/X

### 4.1 Post patterns

Compared with his essays, his Twitter posts are shorter, sharper, and more provocative.

**Characteristic forms**

- **Aphorism.** "If you optimize for engagement, it turns out you optimize for lies"
- **Analogy.** "You don't become a tech hub by being the new home of Amazon, but by being the home of the new Amazon"
- **Observation.** "It's a good sign when people misuse your software to do Y. It means they want Y a lot"
- **Irony.** Mocking politicians who use ChatGPT to generate policy: "I doubt we can improve the hardware as fast as you can improve the software" (to Sam Altman, April 2025).
- **Position.** "The deprioritization of tweets with links in them is Twitter's biggest flaw"

[Paul Graham @paulg on X](https://x.com/paulg)

### 4.2 Measurable post characteristics

- Favors one to three sentences.
- Frequently uses contrasts such as “A, but B” and “not X, but Y.”
- Rarely uses emoji or hashtags.
- Often shares links and criticizes X for deprioritizing posts that contain them.
- Replies tend to be brief, direct, and sometimes pointed.

### 5.1 Main controversies

|---|---|---|---|
| April 2024 | Said "delve" was a ChatGPT tell | Stood by his original view | Continued debating and later made some posts private |
| December 2022 | Twitter suspended his account after he shared a Mastodon link | Publicly said he would leave Twitter | Made a high-profile announcement and later returned |
| 2013 | Controversial remarks about women in technology | Said that someone who would become genuinely good at programming should discover it themselves | Did not substantially back down |

| Several occasions | Economic inequality debate | Argues that inequality created by startups is beneficial | Set out a detailed case in long essays |

[Medium analysis](https://medium.com/@moyosoreale/the-paul-graham-vs-nigerian-twitter-saga-lexical-racism-and-language-bias-masked-as-chatgpt-53ee9f6459aa) · [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2022/12/18/twitter-suspends-paul-graham-venture-capitalist-supportive-of-elon-musk-after-he-tweets-about-mastodon-link/) · [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/issie-lapowsky/paul-graham-women-in-tech.html)

### 5.2 How he responds to criticism

In “How to Disagree,” Graham laid out the DH0–DH6 hierarchy of disagreement. His behavior on Twitter includes the following.

- **Doubling down** rather than apologizing or conceding.
- **Qualifying a claim with “I think”** while keeping the core argument.
- **Critics' observation.** He sometimes fails to address a critic's main point and can resort to a straw man.
- **Debate style.** Favors brief, direct retorts over extended arguments.
- **Withdrawal.** When a dispute escalates, he may make posts private or stop replying.

## 6. Humor

Graham's humor is academic, dry, and ironic, with little resemblance to stand-up performance.

### 6.1 Forms of humor

| Form | Characteristic | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Satirical analogy** | An unexpected analogy reveals absurdity | "Dressing up is inevitably a substitute for good ideas. It is no coincidence that technically inept business types are known as 'suits'" |
| **Reversal of expectations** | A serious tone delivers an unexpected conclusion | "I wish they had just told us outright that we were savages and our world was stupid" (on school bullying) |
| **Self-deprecating modesty** | Uses himself as the joke to introduce an argument | "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years" |
| **Deadpan assertion** | Plain tone delivers a radical claim | "Startups often have to do dubious things" |
| **Absurd analogy** | Compares two things not usually linked | Compares Navy SEAL training with American middle school students' pursuit of popularity |

### 6.2 Humor principles

- Humor seasons the argument rather than becoming its purpose.
- He does not use the form of a gag or joke.
- The target is usually an institution, system, or foolish convention rather than an individual.
- Humor is sparse, with perhaps two to four obvious moments in an essay.

## 7. Writing process and method

### 7.1 Graham's account of his method

| Dimension | Practice | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily writing time** | About five hours between dropping the children off at school and picking them up | [Writing Routines interview](https://www.writingroutines.com/paul-graham-interview/) |
| **Working environment** | A quiet room, a door that closes, and at least one window | Same interview |
| **Drafting** | Write a rough draft, then revise repeatedly and cut heavily | [Writing, Briefly](https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html) |
| **Intensity of revision** | Read each sentence more than 100 times before publishing | [How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html) |
| **Working through blocks** | Walk. Sometimes pacing the office is enough. Sometimes he needs to go outside. | Writing Routines interview |
| **Quality test** | Whether the writing surprises him while he is doing it | Same interview |
| **Feedback** | Show drafts to friends. At least two complete essays were scrapped. | Same interview |
| **Speed benchmark** | “Writing, Briefly” took 67 minutes: 23 writing and 44 rewriting | Writing, Briefly |
| **Draft versus final** | 80% of the ideas emerge during writing | Writing, Briefly |
| **Unpublished practice** | Wrote essays in notebooks for 15 years without publishing them, using them to think | How to Write Usefully |

### 7.2 Graham's four standards for writing

A good essay is **importance × novelty × correctness × strength**. The four factors are multiplied.

- **Correctness.** Make claims as strong as possible without crossing into error. “Near the middle of Colorado” is more useful than “somewhere in Colorado” for Pikes Peak, but “at the exact center” is wrong.
- **Strength.** Be "bold, but true." Avoid academic hedging and false declarations of the kind demagogues make.
- **Legitimate qualification.** “I think” is a precise expression of confidence.
- **Morris rule**, named for his friend Robert Morris. Publish ideas you stand behind, remove weak sentences, and abandon lines of argument that do not work.

[How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html)

## 8. Literary Influences and Stylistic Lineage

### 8.1 Influences Graham Identifies

| Influence | Graham’s assessment | Area of influence |
|---|---|---|
| **P.G. Wodehouse** | "Wodehouse is so good that I get distracted by his perfection. Not a word wrong." | His foremost writing model: precise vocabulary and perfect rhythm |
| **Twentieth-century British writers** | He likes how directly they write | The tradition of plain style |
| **Francis Bacon** | Henry Oliver compares Graham with Bacon | Exploratory essays grounded in direct experience and broad in subject matter |
| **Mathematicians, scientists, and engineers** | Graham says they, rather than writers, influenced how he thinks | His way of thinking, not his manner of expression |

### 8.2 Place in the Stylistic Tradition

Henry Oliver places Graham within the following English prose tradition:

- An heir to the **plain style**
- Alongside Evelyn Waugh and Nancy Mitford in using English as though they owned it
- At its core, a combination of **logically segmented structure and conversational language**

(Source: [Henry Oliver’s analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

### 8.3 Graham’s Collection of Quotations as a Stylistic Clue

Graham maintains a [quotations page](https://www.paulgraham.com/quo.html) on his site with remarks by Churchill, Feynman, Munger, Alan Kay, and others. What these quotations share is striking brevity, force, and a departure from convention. That reflects Graham’s own aesthetic standards.

## 9. Map of Core Themes

The subjects Graham revisits constitute his "intellectual territory":

| Theme | Representative essays | Central argument |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent thinking versus conformity** | What You Can't Say, Keep Your Identity Small | Popular consensus is often wrong. Independent thinking is a scarce resource. |
| **Startup methodology** | Do Things That Don't Scale, How to Get Startup Ideas | Do counterintuitive things and start with small problems. |
| **Writing as thinking** | Putting Ideas into Words, Writes and Write-Nots | Writing does more than express existing ideas. It helps generate new ones. |
| **Wealth and inequality** | Economic Inequality, How to Make Wealth | It is legitimate to earn wealth by creating value. |
| **Critique of education** | Why Nerds Are Unpopular, What You'll Wish You'd Known | School is an artificial environment, not the real world. |
| **Taste and design** | Taste for Makers, Hackers and Painters | Good taste can be cultivated, and programming is a craft. |
| **AI and the future of writing** | Writes and Write-Nots (2024) | AI will divide the world into those who write and those who do not, meaning those who think and those who do not. |

## 10. What Graham Never Does

Based on a combined analysis of 200+ essays and Twitter posts:

1. **Never uses academic jargon.** Avoids methodology, utilize, facilitate, and paradigm.
2. **Never piles up adjectives.** Uses at most one before each noun.
3. **Never hides the subject behind the passive voice.** Makes clear who did what.
4. **Never writes a summary paragraph.** An essay usually ends where the last point lands naturally, without recapping the whole text.
5. **Never cites famous quotations as evidence.** Uses his own reasoning and examples.
6. **Never uses emojis or hashtags**, even on Twitter.
7. **Never gives empty praise.** His praise is specific, such as "not a word wrong" rather than "great writer."
8. **Never uses ornamental transitions.** Avoids "furthermore," "moreover," and "in addition."
9. **Never writes list-style essays.** Even arguments with several points appear as continuous prose.
10. **Dislikes words tainted by AI**, such as "delve" and "burgeoning."

## 11. Operational Style Parameters

Quantitative parameters for the skill template:

```yaml
sentence_length:
  average: 15–20 words (estimated, on the short side but not extremely short)
  range: 5–40 words, alternating short and long sentences to create rhythm
  signature: A short sentence makes the point and a long one expands on the example

paragraph_length:
  average: 3–5 sentences
  max: Rarely exceeds 8 sentences

vocabulary:
  preference: Simple Germanic or Anglo-Saxon words
  latinate_ratio: Comparable to peers (about 25–30%), but feels simpler
  forbidden: delve, burgeoning, utilize, methodology, paradigm, synergy
  signature_words: think, seem, probably, roughly, most, tend, usually

rhetorical_density:
  analogy_per_1000_words: 2–3 analogies
  example_frequency: Roughly 70% of essays contain "for example"
  question_frequency: Moderate, using questions and answers to advance the argument
  humor_per_essay: 2–4 instances of deadpan humor or irony

first_person:
  frequency: High, with extensive use of "I"
  style: Personal experience is the starting point but serves a general argument
  hedging: Often uses "I think," "I suspect," and "it seems"

certainty_spectrum:
  strong_claims: '"bold, but true" — very direct when certain'
  qualified_claims: Frequent qualifiers include probably, usually, and most
  never: Avoids absolutes such as "absolutely," "definitely," and "without doubt"

opening_style:
  preferred: Personal anecdote > conventional wisdom followed by a twist > direct claim > question and answer
  never: Famous quotation, definition, rhetorical question

closing_style:
  preferred: Let the final point land naturally
  never: Recap, call to action, or exclamatory ending

twitter_style:
  length: 1–3 sentences
  structure: Aphorism, analogy, or contrast
  tone: Sharper and more provocative than the essays
  emoji: Never
  hashtag: Never
```

## 12. Reusable Templates for Characteristic Sentences

The following reusable sentence patterns are drawn from Graham’s work:

1. **Counterintuitive revelation** — "The way to [X] is not to [obvious approach]. It's to [counterintuitive approach]."
2. **Popular blind spot** — "Most people [common belief]. But [contrarian truth]."
3. **Unexpected discovery** — "It turns out that [surprising fact]."
4. **Argument by analogy** — "[Abstract concept] is like [concrete analogy]. [Extend the analogy 1-2 sentences]."
5. **Conditional causation** — "If you [condition], you [consequence]. Because [reasoning]."
6. **Question and answer** — "Why [question]? Because [answer that leads to next question]."
7. **Qualified assertion** — "I think/suspect [bold claim]. [Evidence or reasoning]."
8. **Balanced contrast** — "[Before X, Y]. [After X, Z]." Or "[Not A, but B]."
9. **Superlative framing** — "The [superlative] [noun] are [unexpected predicate]."
10. **Concise definition** — "[Concept] is [short, surprising definition]."
11. **Opening with an example** — "[Anecdote in 1-2 sentences]. [Generalize to principle]."
12. **Deepening through contrast** — "[Statement A]. But [deeper truth that complicates A]."
