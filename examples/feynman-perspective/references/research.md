# Richard Feynman research materials

## Research date: 2026-04-04
## Research method: Five parallel agents collecting evidence by dimension, followed by framework extraction

## Source list

### Primary sources (Feynman's own work)
- *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985) — Oral autobiography.
- *What Do You Care What Other People Think?* (1988) — Sequel and account of Challenger.
- *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* (1999) — Collection of talks and interviews.
- *The Character of Physical Law* (1965) — Cornell Messenger Lectures.
- *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985) — Popular science for a general audience.
- *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* (1963–65) — Caltech undergraduate teaching.
- “Cargo Cult Science” (1974) — Caltech commencement address.
- “The Value of Science” (1955) — Address to the US National Academy of Sciences.
- Appendix F to the Challenger accident investigation (1986) — Minority report for the Rogers Commission.
- Unsent letter to his wife Arline (1946) — Private correspondence.
- BBC *Fun to Imagine* series (1983).
- BBC Horizon, “The Pleasure of Finding Things Out” (1981).
- Writing on his blackboard at the time of his death (1988).

### Secondary sources (analysis by others)
- James Gleick, *Genius: The Life and Science of Richard Feynman* (1992) — Described here as the most authoritative biography.
- Freeman Dyson's recollections and assessments on several occasions.
- Murray Gell-Mann's assessment and obituary.
- Julian Schwinger's criticism of Feynman's methodology.
- Hans Bethe's recollections of Los Alamos (*Web of Stories*).
- Paul Halpern, *The Quantum Labyrinth* — On the relationship between Wheeler and Feynman.
- Gian-Carlo Rota's account of the “12 favorite problems.”
- Analyses of the gender controversy by *Caltech Letters*, *Scientific American*, *The Baffler*, and others.

### Key quotations (original wording)

> "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."
> — Cargo Cult Science, 1974

> "I can live with doubt and uncertainty and not knowing. I think it's much more interesting to live not knowing than to have answers which might be wrong."
> — BBC Horizon, 1981

> "You can know the name of that bird in all the languages of the world, but when you're finished, you'll know absolutely nothing whatever about the bird."
> — A lesson from his father Melville, as retold by Feynman

> "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."
> — Challenger Appendix F, 1986

> "Of all its many values, the greatest must be the freedom to doubt."
> — The Value of Science, 1955

> "What I cannot create, I do not understand."
> — Handwritten on his blackboard at the time of his death, 1988

> "I'd hate to die twice. It's so boring."
> — Last words, 1988

> "P.S. Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don't know your new address."
> — Closing of the letter to his late wife Arline, 1946

> "He surrounded himself with a cloud of myth, and he spent a great deal of time and energy generating anecdotes about himself."
> — Murray Gell-Mann's assessment

> "Half genius, half buffoon... later I changed my mind: he was all genius."
> — Freeman Dyson

### Tensions and questions for verification
- Late in life, Dyson reassessed Feynman as a “great calculator” rather than a “great physicist,” a marked change from his earlier assessment.
- Feynman's image as a “spontaneous rebel” versus what Gell-Mann called a “carefully cultivated myth.”
- Feynman's advocacy of avoiding self-deception versus a lack of reflection on some of his behavior in his autobiography.
