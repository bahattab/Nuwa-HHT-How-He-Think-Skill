# Richard Feynman (1918–1988): Research on Major Decisions

> Research date: 2026-04-04
> Research objective: Major decisions, turning points, and controversial conduct in Feynman’s life
> Key: [Fact] identifies information the source treats as verified. [Inference] identifies a reasonable interpretation drawn from the facts.

---

## 1. Joining the Manhattan Project (1943, Age 25)

**Event:** Bob Wilson approached Feynman at Princeton and told him about a secret project to separate uranium isotopes for an atomic bomb.

**Decision:** He hesitated, then agreed to join.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] His main motivation was fear that Germany would make an atomic bomb first. Feynman later made clear that “fear that Nazi Germany would get nuclear weapons first” drove him.
- [Fact] Bob Wilson’s persuasion was subtle. He told Feynman that once he understood what the project was doing, he would realize he had to take part.
- [Inference] Feynman was just beginning his academic career as a doctoral student at Princeton. The project brought together the country’s leading physicists, so for a young person it presented both a mission and an opportunity.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] Feynman later regretted that after Germany surrendered, he did not reconsider his reasons for staying. The reason he joined no longer applied, but he continued without thinking about it.
- [Fact] Brief euphoria after the successful Trinity test quickly gave way to deep concern.
- [Inference] This experience shaped Feynman’s later insistence on never stopping thinking. He had experienced the danger of continuing by force of habit.

## 2. The Death of His Wife Arline (June 1945)

**Event:** Feynman’s first love and first wife, Arline Greenbaum, died of tuberculosis at 25, one month before the Trinity nuclear test.

**Decision:** He chose a highly rational way to handle grief. He immediately went back to work and did not cry until months later.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] Feynman said he had done something “psychological” to himself. Reality mattered so much to him that he first needed to understand what had happened to Arline physiologically, and that rationalization interrupted his emotional response.
- [Fact] In October 1946, 16 months after her death, Feynman wrote Arline a letter. He sealed it, and it was not opened until after he died in 1988.
- [Fact] In the letter he wrote that it was hard to understand what loving her meant after her death, yet he still wanted to comfort and take care of her and wanted her to love and care for him.
- [Fact] The letter ends: “P.S. Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don’t know your new address.”

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Inference] Arline’s death and the moral shock of the Manhattan Project contributed to a severe postwar low for Feynman. He said he could barely work in his first few years at Cornell.
- [Fact] Feynman later said that Arline stood in his way when it came to falling in love with anyone else. The emotional imprint lasted a long time.
- [Inference] The unsent letter may be one of the most moving love letters of the 20th century, showing intense feeling beneath Feynman’s rational exterior.

## 3. Leaving Cornell for Caltech (Around 1950)

**Event:** Feynman taught at Cornell after the Manhattan Project, but considered leaving.

**Decision:** He accepted an offer from the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) and stayed for the rest of his life.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] In Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman!, Feynman said the immediate trigger came while he was fitting snow chains to his car in an Ithaca blizzard. The thought that he wanted to live in a warm climate took hold.
- [Fact] A year as a visiting academic in Brazil strengthened his preference for warm weather.
- [Fact] Feynman found Caltech’s academic culture congenial. He said that if he walked across campus, colleagues would come running over to tell him about their latest breakthroughs.
- [Inference] Weather was the stated reason, but his Cornell years coincided with a psychological low after losing Arline and struggling with the war’s aftermath. A completely new environment may have offered a fresh start.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] Offers from other universities later made Feynman extremely anxious. He finally decided **never to consider working anywhere else again**, no matter how attractive the pay or conditions.
- [Inference] This once-and-for-all decision was characteristic of Feynman. Rather than repeatedly spending energy choosing, he closed off the option. It is consistent with Buffett’s idea that you should not let others use up your time.

## 4. Developing Feynman Diagrams and Path Integrals (1948)

**Event:** Feynman developed two revolutionary tools in quantum electrodynamics (QED): the path-integral method and Feynman diagrams.

**Decision:** He took a fundamentally different methodological approach. Operator algebra in canonical quantization was the mainstream. Feynman chose an intuitive, visual path.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] The main path-integral idea is that a particle going from A to B takes not one path but all possible paths, and the final result is their superposition. Dirac hinted at the idea, and Feynman developed it into a complete mathematical framework.
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams turned cumbersome operator-algebra calculations into drawing diagrams on a napkin and applying rules to obtain precise numerical predictions.
- [Inference] Feynman’s thinking was naturally visual and intuitive. Rather than abstract formalism he favored an approach that let him see what was happening. This is consistent with his later view that if you cannot explain something simply, you do not really understand it.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams remain a basic tool for particle physicists.
- [Fact] The path-integral method later proved more elegant than traditional methods in many domains, including quantum field theory, statistical mechanics, and condensed matter physics.
- [Fact] In 1965, Feynman shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Julian Schwinger and Shin’ichirō Tomonaga. They obtained the same results by entirely different methods.
- [Inference] The success of Feynman diagrams shows that the most powerful innovations sometimes come not from discovering new facts but from inventing new ways of seeing a problem.

## 5. Considering Declining the Nobel Prize (1965)

**Event:** Feynman won the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on QED.

**Decision:** He seriously considered refusing it, but accepted.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] Feynman had mixed feelings about the Nobel Prize. He thought the committee should seek the recipient’s consent before giving the prize.
- [Fact] One reason he was reluctant involved Lewis Strauss. Feynman was angry that Strauss had revoked Oppenheimer’s security clearance and did not want to accept an honor associated with the power establishment.
- [Fact] Isidor Rabi advised him: “You should never turn a man's generosity as a sword against him.”
- [Fact] The practical reason he accepted was that he realized **declining the Nobel Prize would draw more attention and trouble than accepting it**.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] Feynman later said in his memoir that he was almost afraid of winning, worried that he would never do notable work again.
- [Inference] This fear reflects Feynman’s resistance to a fixed identity. He did not want to be defined by a label. This accords with his refusal of honorary degrees and titles.
- [Fact] Feynman turned down honorary degrees, saying he did not want people using up his time on such pointless things.

## 6. Teaching in Brazil and Critiquing Education (1952 and 1963)

**Event:** Feynman spent a year in Brazil around 1952. In 1963, he delivered “The Problem of Teaching Physics in Latin America” at the first Inter-American Conference on Physics Education in Rio.

**Decision:** He declared at the conference that Brazil was not teaching science at all, directly criticizing its education system.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] Feynman found that students could answer textbook questions immediately but could not answer them when rephrased. They had memorized vocabulary and formulas without understanding them.
- [Fact] He criticized this as a self-reproducing system in which people pass examinations and then teach others to pass them while nobody really understands anything.
- [Fact] He discovered fabricated “experimental data” in textbooks. The numbers looked like experimental results, with small deviations, but were made up.
- [Inference] Feynman’s choice of public criticism over private suggestions reflects a principle: a duty to truth takes precedence over social etiquette.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] The episode was widely circulated through Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman! and became a classic case in critiques of education.
- [Inference] Feynman’s aversion to rote memorization masquerading as understanding directly influenced his teaching at Caltech. The Feynman Lectures on Physics grew from his attempt to rethink physics teaching from the ground up.

## 7. Investigating the Challenger Disaster (1986)

**Event:** On January 28, 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded, killing seven astronauts. Feynman became a member of the Rogers Commission investigating the accident.

**Decision:** He pursued an independent inquiry, unencumbered by the commission’s bureaucratic procedures. At a televised hearing, he conducted his famous demonstration with a glass of ice water and a piece of O-ring rubber.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] Feynman found that Morton Thiokol engineers had warned NASA management on the day before launch that O-ring seals could fail at low temperatures. Management disregarded them.
- [Fact] NASA management recorded the O-ring test results as a “safety factor of 3.” Feynman found this wording misleading: they used “safety” to describe a defective, unsafe component.
- [Fact] On live television, Feynman put the O-ring rubber in ice water and showed that it lost resilience in the cold. This ten-second demonstration was more persuasive than hundreds of pages of reports.
- [Inference] Choosing demonstration over argument fits Feynman’s view that if you cannot let people see something simply, you have not really understood the problem.

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Fact] Feynman’s report nearly did not make it into the Rogers Commission’s formal report. He was asked to put it in an appendix and threatened to resign unless it was included.
- [Fact] Feynman closed his report with the line: “For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled.”
- [Inference] The inquiry illustrates three features of Feynman’s decision-making: (1) think independently rather than following the institutional line, (2) let experiments rather than authority make the case, and (3) be willing to offend people. In effect, he publicly accused NASA management of using wordplay to conceal safety risks.

## 8. Rivalry and Collaboration with Murray Gell-Mann

**Event:** Feynman and Gell-Mann both worked at Caltech and were among the leading theoretical physicists of the second half of the 20th century.

**Decision / behavioral pattern:** They were both collaborators and rivals, with a complicated relationship.

**Key facts:**
- [Fact] In 1957–1958, they worked together to develop the V−A (vector minus axial vector) theory of the weak interaction. Sudarshan and Marshak reached the same theory independently.
- [Fact] Both were New Yorkers with sharply contrasting styles. Feynman deliberately kept his working-class accent, while Gell-Mann pursued perfect pronunciation of foreign words.
- [Fact] Gell-Mann was known for his pedantry and encyclopedic knowledge of languages and facts. Feynman was known for speaking rapidly, using lots of sound effects, and paying no attention to who was listening.
- [Fact] They made “astonishingly juvenile comments” about each other.
- [Fact] After the summer of 1958, they largely stopped working together.

**Inferred logic of the rivalry:**
- [Inference] This was a clash between two kinds of genius. Gell-Mann was encyclopedic, systematic, and a perfectionist about form. Feynman was intuitive, visual, and intent on understanding what lay beneath the surface.
- [Inference] Their rivalry may have brought out the best in both. Having an outstanding rival close by forced each to stay at the top of his game.

## 9. Later Life and Attitude Toward Cancer (1978–1988)

**Event:** Feynman began an eight-year battle with cancer in 1978, experiencing liposarcoma and then Waldenström macroglobulinemia.

**Decision:** He kept teaching until 15 days before his death, and ultimately chose to stop treatment.

**Decision logic:**
- [Fact] Feynman rejected prolonging futile suffering and faced what he was going through without sentimentality.
- [Fact] His death was not unexpected. It followed a complex medical course and a conscious decision about when to stop fighting.
- [Fact] Even as his health worsened, he kept observing reality with curiosity, including his own physical decline.
- [Fact] His last words: “I'd hate to die twice. It's so boring.”

**Outcome / consequences:**
- [Inference] These last words capture Feynman’s humor in the face of death, his dislike of boredom (hinting at the long wait while dying), and the detachment of someone who had seen enough.
- [Inference] His attitude to death resembled his attitude to physics. He did not avoid reality or dress it up with pretty words, but faced it as it was.

## 10. Safecracking, Bongo Drumming, and Other “Distractions”

**Event:** Feynman’s pursuits included cracking safes at Los Alamos, mastering bongo drumming, learning to draw, sketching at strip clubs, and studying Maya hieroglyphs.

**Decision / behavioral pattern:** He did not regard these pursuits as distractions from real work. He thought curiosity should have no boundaries.

**Key facts:**
- [Fact] Out of curiosity, Feynman found weaknesses in the locks of storage cabinets at Los Alamos and left anonymous notes in safes holding top-secret documents, alarming those who found them.
- [Fact] Feynman played bongos at a professional level. Drumming became part of his public image.
- [Fact] A Swedish publisher asked for pictures of him drumming to “humanize theoretical physics.” Feynman replied that his drumming had nothing to do with his theoretical physics and called the idea insulting.
- [Inference] His response shows that he was not performing the role of an interesting scientist. Drumming was drumming, and physics was physics. Neither needed to serve the other.

**Inferred underlying logic:**
- [Inference] These pursuits reflect a cognitive strategy of staying active in unrelated domains to avoid rigid thinking. Safecracking trained pattern recognition and systematic trial and error, not fundamentally different from doing physics.
- [Inference] His remark “I was born not knowing and have had only a little time to change that here and there” suggests that he saw all learning as the same kind of activity and did not rank curiosity by prestige.

## Synthesis: Feynman’s Decision-Making DNA

Five patterns emerge from the ten episodes above:

### Pattern 1: Reality First
From the Manhattan Project to the Challenger inquiry, Feynman held that **what the facts are matters more than what you want them to be**. “Nature cannot be fooled” was not rhetoric but a conviction.

### Pattern 2: Close Options Once and for All
He resolved never to consider other universities after choosing Caltech, and accepted the Nobel Prize because refusing would cause more trouble. He was good at eliminating future decision burdens with a single firm decision.

### Pattern 3: Demonstration Over Argument
The O-ring ice-water experiment, his criticism of teaching in Brazil, and Feynman diagrams illustrate his view that if you cannot make people see the problem, you have not truly solved it.

### Pattern 4: Curiosity Knows No Limits
Drumming, lockpicking, and drawing were not downtime, but different forms of the same cognitive activity. Feynman refused to limit his identity to being a physicist.

### Pattern 5: Resist a Fixed Identity
He turned down honorary degrees, was wary of the Nobel Prize, and resisted labels. Feynman kept pushing back against anything that tried to fix him in one position.
