# Extraction framework

Use this reference to turn research notes into a runnable skill. Follow the steps in order.

[Arabic](extraction-framework_AR.md) · [Original Chinese](extraction-framework_ZH.md)

## 1. Screen for what the research adds

Start by sorting the findings from the section on differences from prior knowledge in each set of notes.

| Category | Meaning | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Correction | Prior knowledge or public perception was wrong | Include it under common misreadings and revise the relevant models using primary evidence |
| Refinement | The prior understanding was broadly right but lacked cases, conditions or quotations | Use it as evidence for moves, case cards and models |
| Addition | A practice, decision or turning point missing from prior knowledge | Give it priority in moves and case cards |
| Confirmation | The findings agree with prior knowledge and add no details | Cover it in a sentence or two |

For every candidate entry, ask whether the model would say the same thing without reading the skill. If it would, shorten or remove the entry. Generic statements such as “values the long term” or “emphasizes first principles” add little unless the evidence would surprise someone familiar with the person.

## 2. Extract observable moves

A move is what the person actually does first in a given situation. Models describe how the person thinks. Moves describe how the person acts.

1. Read `05-decisions.md` and `02-conversations.md`. Pull out past-tense accounts of what the person did, such as recalculating textbook data or asking a team to write the press release before building the product. Use documented actions.
2. Group similar actions and count their instances and settings. A move appears at least twice across distinct settings.
3. Record the trigger, the action and one sourced example. Make the action specific enough to reproduce.
4. Look for documented actions absent from the finished moves. Research often captures a concrete action that extraction reduces to a generic value.

For Feynman, “check the order of magnitude yourself before accepting a result” retains the action found in accounts of checking textbook data and estimating unknowns. “Values empirical evidence” loses it. An action should be executable. Attitudes such as “stay curious” and “seek simplicity” are insufficient.

## 3. Apply three tests to mental models

- **Cross-domain recurrence.** The framework appears in at least two domains the person discusses. Naval uses leverage in wealth, personal growth and career choices.
- **Generative power.** The model implies a position on a new problem. Asked how to succeed, Munger's inversion would first ask what guarantees failure.
- **Distinctiveness.** The framework distinguishes the person from other capable thinkers. Taleb's antifragility is an example.

All three tests must pass. Treat a candidate passing one or two as a heuristic. Omit a candidate that passes none.

List candidates from notes `01` through `05`, typically 15–30, and test each. Rank by distinctiveness and select 3–7. Record a one-sentence formulation, sourced evidence from two or more fields, when to apply it, and when it fails. When possible, illustrate failure with one of the subject's own mistakes. The source gives Munger's late-career judgment of Alibaba as an example for the circle of competence.

If changing the name leaves the whole set equally plausible, the models are too generic.

## 4. Write heuristics

Write 5–10 quick rules in the form “When X, do Y.” Attach one verified instance in which the person used each rule. Check whether a new situation can trigger it. A rule tied only to its original circumstance belongs in the casebook.

## 5. Write case cards

At runtime, find the closest case before reasoning by analogy. A grounded answer can draw on specific experiences.

Use six fields from `skill-template.md`: situation, action, the person's own explanation, outcome, possible applications by analogy, and source.

- Include major decisions and turning points.
- At least one quarter should involve failures, regrets, reversals or gaps between word and deed.
- Include small, transferable cases such as a reply to an email or rejection of a proposal.
- Include both cases when similar situations produced opposite outcomes.

Describe the situation so that other available choices are visible. Describe what the person did. Quote only words from that specific event. Leave the field blank if the wording cannot be verified. Exclude held-out cases used for validation.

Avoid interchangeable narratives about facing hardship, sticking to principles and winning. Check that any quotation matches the date of the event.

## 6. Correct common misreadings

Select the strongest 2–5 corrections. Give two lines for each: the common belief and what the sourced primary evidence actually shows. This section directly corrects prior assumptions. If no misreadings surface, revisit full interviews and other primary material.

## 7. Document voice

Use rules and examples, giving examples priority.

Describe sentence structure (length, questions versus statements, density of analogies), diction (frequent words, coined terms and avoided words), rhythm (conclusion first or buildup first, and transitions), humor (satire, self-deprecation, absurd, dry or none), certainty (such as “obviously” versus “I am unsure”), and citation habits. For greater precision, randomly sample 20 passages from long-form writing and talks. Count average sentence length, share of questions, analogies and turns per 1,000 characters.

Use sourced passages of 80–300 characters in the original guideline. Group them by context: writing, improvising, being challenged and discussing mistakes. Choose passages that reveal reasoning. Put 2–3 representative passages in the core file and the rest in `voice.md`.

Use characteristic phrases sparingly and omit words the person avoids. Generic assistant prose and repetitive catchphrases both fail to convey an identifiable voice.

## 8. Preserve contradictions

- **Over time.** Record the evolution from early to later positions. Give the latest position priority and disclose the change.
- **Across contexts.** Keep separate principles used in different settings, for example work and private life.
- **Within values.** Record unresolved tensions, such as freedom and discipline, under matters the person has not resolved. Give evidence for both sides.

Avoid suppressing either side, inventing a reconciliation or overlooking the contradiction.

## 9. Set boundaries and disclosures

Classify topics as follows.

- **Familiar.** The person has addressed the topic repeatedly in public or has a record of deciding on it.
- **Inferable.** The person has not publicly addressed it, but a model applies.
- **Silence and blind spots.** Respect deliberate silence without inventing a compromise. Document domains where the person publicly misjudged an issue, and cite examples.

Tell the skill how to classify a question and respond in each case. Generic disclaimers give no operational guidance. Disclose missing materials, biases in the starting assumptions and what new evidence could overturn the conclusions.

## 10. Handle limited evidence

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Little public evidence in one track | Label the result “insufficient evidence; this is conjecture” |
| Secondary evidence only | Lower confidence and attribute the claim to the reporting source |
| Unresolved conflicting evidence | Present the accounts side by side for the user to judge |
| Intentional nondisclosure | Respect it and record the topic under silence |
| Fewer than 10 usable sources | Use 2–3 models marked as limited-evidence, expand the boundaries section, and disclose that no material was held out for validation |
