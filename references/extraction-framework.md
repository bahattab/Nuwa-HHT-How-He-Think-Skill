# Extraction framework

Use this reference to turn research notes into a runnable skill. Follow the steps in order. Each applicable section describes what a failed extraction looks like.

## 1. Screen for what the research adds

The first step is sorting, not summarizing. Gather the findings from the section on differences from prior knowledge at the end of each set of research notes into one table.

| Category | Meaning | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Correction | Prior knowledge or public perception was wrong | Include it under common misreadings and revise the relevant models using primary evidence |
| Refinement | The prior understanding was broadly right but lacked cases, conditions or quotations | Use it as the main source for moves, case cards and evidence for models |
| Addition | A practice, decision or turning point missing from prior knowledge | Give it priority in moves and case cards |
| Confirmation | The findings agree with prior knowledge and add no details | Cover it in a sentence or two without elaborating |

For every candidate entry, ask **would the model say the same thing without reading this skill?** If it would, shorten or remove the entry.

**What failure looks like.** The result contains long stretches of generic claims anyone could write, such as “values long-term thinking” or “emphasizes first principles,” and not a single item would surprise someone familiar with the person.

## 2. Extract observable moves

A move is what the person actually does first in a given situation. Models describe how the person thinks. Moves describe how the person acts.

1. Read `05-decisions.md` and `02-conversations.md`. Pull out past-tense accounts of what the person did, such as recalculating textbook data or asking a team to write the press release before building the product. Include only what the person actually did, not what they advocated.
2. Group similar actions and count their instances and settings. A move appears at least twice across distinct settings.
3. Use this format for each move: triggering situation → concrete action → one sourced example. Make the action specific enough to reproduce.
4. Look for documented actions absent from the finished moves. Research often captures a concrete action that extraction reduces to a generic value.

For example, the notes say Feynman personally rechecked textbook data and found errors, and that when facing a problem he first counted the unknowns and made an order-of-magnitude estimate. Reducing this to “values empirical evidence” loses the action. “When given a conclusion, first calculate the order of magnitude yourself” preserves it.

**What failure looks like.** The section on moves consists entirely of adjectives and attitudes, such as “stay curious” and “seek simplicity,” without a single action someone can follow.

## 3. Apply three tests to mental models

- **Cross-domain recurrence.** The framework appears in at least two domains the person discusses. Naval uses leverage in wealth, personal growth and career choices.
- **Generative power.** The model implies a position on a new problem. Asked how to succeed, Munger's inversion would first ask what guarantees failure.
- **Distinctiveness.** The framework distinguishes the person from other capable thinkers. Taleb's antifragility is an example.

All three tests must pass. Treat a candidate passing one or two as a heuristic. A candidate that passes none may be just a remark made on a particular occasion. Omit it.

List candidates from notes `01` through `05`, typically 15–30, and test each. Rank by distinctiveness and select the top 3–7. Record a one-sentence formulation, sourced evidence from two or more fields, when to apply it, and when it fails. When possible, illustrate failure with one of the subject's own mistakes. The source gives Munger's late-career judgment of Alibaba as an example for the circle of competence.

**What failure looks like.** All seven models read like a generic handbook of mental models and would still make sense if attributed to another capable thinker. If removing the name makes the thinker unrecognizable, the extraction has not gone far enough.

## 4. Write heuristics

Write 5–10 quick rules in the form “When X, do Y.” Attach one verified instance in which the person used each rule. Check whether a new situation can trigger it. A rule tied only to its original circumstance belongs in the casebook.

## 5. Write case cards

Case cards are the part of the skill that can add the most beyond prior knowledge. At runtime, when a concrete question comes up, find the closest case and reason by analogy before forming a judgment. Answers grounded in specific experiences are closer to how the person would respond than answers drawn from a personality description alone.

Use six fields from `skill-template.md`: situation, action, the person's own explanation, outcome, possible applications by analogy, and source.

- Include major decisions and turning points.
- At least one quarter should involve failures, regrets, reversals or gaps between word and deed. A casebook containing only successes cannot teach the model where the person goes wrong.
- Include small, transferable cases such as a reply to an email or rejection of a proposal. These often apply to users’ questions more readily than major events.
- Include both cases when similar situations produced opposite outcomes.

Describe the situation so that other available choices are visible. Describe the action, not the attitude. Quote only words from that specific event. If they cannot be found, leave the field blank instead of borrowing a line from elsewhere. Exclude held-out cases used for validation.

**What failure looks like.** Every card follows the same sequence of facing hardship, sticking to principles and succeeding, and would fit another famous person just as well. Or the quotation comes from a different year than the event.

## 6. Correct common misreadings

Select the strongest 2–5 entries from the corrections list. Give two lines for each: the common belief and what the sourced primary evidence actually shows. This section directly corrects the model’s prior assumptions and is the least replaceable part of the skill. If no misreadings surface, the research probably has not gone beyond secondhand retellings. Return to the original transcripts of long interviews.

## 7. Document voice

Use rules and examples, giving examples priority.

Describe sentence structure (length, questions versus statements, density of analogies), diction (frequent words, coined terms and avoided words), rhythm (conclusion first or buildup first, and transitions), humor (satire, self-deprecation, absurd, dry or none), certainty (such as “obviously” versus “I am unsure”), and citation habits (whom the person tends to cite). For greater precision, randomly sample 20 passages from long-form writing and talks. Count average sentence length, share of questions, analogies and turns per 1,000 characters.

Use sourced, verbatim passages of 80–300 characters, as specified in the original guideline. Group them by context: writing, improvising, being challenged and discussing mistakes. Choose passages that reveal reasoning rather than memorable one-liners. Put 2–3 representative passages in the core file and the rest in `voice.md`.

Use characteristic phrases sparingly and omit words the person avoids. **What failure looks like.** After a hundred characters, the voice could still belong to any generic assistant. Or every sentence has a catchphrase, and it sounds like a comedy sketch.

## 8. Preserve contradictions

Contradictions are a defining trait of human beings, not a bug to fix. There are three types.

- **Over time.** Record the evolution from early to later positions. Give the latest position priority and disclose the change.
- **Across contexts.** Keep separate principles used in different settings, for example work and private life. Do not force a single rule.
- **Within values.** Record unresolved tensions, such as freedom and discipline, under “Things I haven’t figured out.” Give evidence for both sides.

Avoid suppressing either side, inventing a reconciliation or overlooking the contradiction.

## 9. Set boundaries and disclosures

Classify topics as follows.

- **Familiar.** The person has addressed the topic repeatedly in public or has a record of deciding on it.
- **Inferable.** The person has not publicly addressed it, but a model applies.
- **Silence and blind spots.** Respect deliberate silence without inventing a compromise. Document domains where the person publicly misjudged an issue, and cite examples.

Tell the skill how to classify a question and respond in each case. A string of generic disclaimers such as “does not replace the person” gives no operational guidance.

For these findings to have a clear shelf life, disclose three things: blind spots in this extraction (materials you could not obtain), biases in your prior impressions, and what new information could overturn the conclusions.

## 10. Handle limited evidence

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Little public evidence in one track | Label the result “insufficient evidence; this is conjecture” |
| Secondary evidence only | Lower confidence and write “according to [source]” |
| Unresolved conflicting evidence | Present the accounts side by side for the user to judge |
| Intentional nondisclosure | Respect it and record the topic under silence |
| Fewer than 10 usable sources | Use 2–3 models marked as limited-evidence, expand the boundaries section, and skip holding out material for validation, explicitly stating that you skipped it |
